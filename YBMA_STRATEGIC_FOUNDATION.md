## 0. Six challenges to the brief

`[INFERENCE]` — these are my professional objections to the brief as written. Most are fixable;
one is load-bearing.

### 0.1 "Everyone" is a fine answer for the master brand and a useless one for marketing

Individuals, startups, SMEs, large companies, government, schools, Nigerian, African and
international clients. For a holding-company brand that must stretch across four sectors,
breadth is correct — the master brand should not be pinned to one buyer. But for the business
that has to invoice someone next month, breadth is paralysis. A website for an individual and a
software system for a state government are different products, prices, sales cycles and proofs.

**You can have a broad brand. You cannot have a broad go-to-market.** One primary revenue
audience is required for the next eighteen months. See Q3.

### 0.2 Versatility is not a benefit

Versatility appears in your list of desired associations. No customer has ever chosen a supplier
because it was versatile. Versatility is a property of your *structure* — it belongs in brand
architecture (section 7), where it is genuinely valuable — not in positioning, where it reads as
"we will do whatever you pay for."

**The group is versatile. Each division is focused.** That distinction is the whole game.

### 0.3 Youthfulness expires

You want YBMA associated with youthfulness, and described the personality as a young African
founder. The problem is arithmetic: in fifteen years you will not be young, and a brand built on
youth will either look dated or look like it is lying.

Worse, youthfulness works against you in the rooms you say you want to enter — government
procurement and enterprise IT buy from firms that look like they will still exist in five years.

**Recommendation:** keep youth as *tempo* — speed, curiosity, appetite, willingness to try
things — and remove it from the brand's aesthetic vocabulary entirely. *Young in tempo, mature
in craft.*

### 0.4 Your five admired brands point five different ways

Apple, Samsung, Microsoft, Dangote and Flutterwave do not point in one direction, and chasing
all five produces mush. The useful reading is one specific lesson from each:

| Brand | What is worth taking | What to leave behind |
|---|---|---|
| **Samsung** | A meaningless name carrying wildly different businesses under one mark. **This is your model.** | Its scale; its consumer-electronics visual language. |
| **Dangote** | Branded-house discipline across cement, sugar and refining; industrial credibility built on delivery, not advertising. | Founder-surname dynamics; heavy-industry tone. |
| **Apple** | Restraint. The willingness to leave things out. Craft as the argument. | Aloofness — Apple can be mysterious because it is famous. You cannot. |
| **Microsoft** | Proof that a single mark can serve individuals, SMEs, enterprise and government simultaneously. | Its four-colour tile; its committee-built visual system. |
| **Flutterwave** | African origin stated plainly, craft standard set globally. **This is the answer to your African-vs-global question, and it is already proven.** | Single-product focus; fintech colour conventions. |

### 0.5 Premium and individual clients pull against each other

You want premium but not inaccessible, and your audience list starts with individuals. In
practice, early-stage firms take cheap work to survive, then quietly lower the standard to make
the margin work, and the brand becomes a claim nobody internally believes.

The way out is not to refuse small work. It is to **scope down instead of discounting**: a small
client gets less scope at the same standard, never the same scope at a lower standard. That is a
rule you will have to enforce on yourselves, and it matters more than any colour palette.

### 0.6 The real risk is not visual

Every differentiator available to you — accountability, transparency, finishing what you start,
work that lasts — is an operational commitment, not a design decision. An identity that says "we
stand behind our work" can be built in two months. Whether that sentence is *true* is decided by
how you behave on a Tuesday in month nine of a difficult project.

**If you are not prepared to change how you operate, say so now**, and the positioning should
instead depend only on craft and taste.

## 1. Brand essence

The essence is the single idea underneath everything — the thing that stays true whether YBMA is
selling software or managing farmland. It is not a slogan. It is the brief you judge every future
decision against.

### E1 — The Signature `[PROPOSED — RECOMMENDED]`

> *"Our names are on it."*

YBMA is four real people's initials. Right now that is the name's weakness — it means nothing to
anyone outside the four of you. This idea inverts it: the name is not an abstraction, it is a
**signature**, and a signature means someone specific is answerable. Every piece of work carries
the personal reputation of a named human being.

- **Communicates:** accountability, personal stake, craftsmanship, seriousness. The opposite of a
  faceless vendor.
- **Strengths:** literally unownable by a competitor, because it is your actual names. Derived
  from a fact rather than an aspiration. Solves the youthful-vs-professional tension by itself —
  young people staking their own names is simultaneously bold and mature. And it gives an unknown
  company the one thing an unknown company can credibly offer: consequence.
- **Weaknesses:** requires you to actually behave accountably; the brand dies loudly if you do
  not. Founder-dependent in the early years, which does not scale past roughly thirty people
  without deliberate systems. If a founder exits, the signature has a ghost in it.
- **Scalability:** very high. A standard travels to any sector. "We put our name on this building"
  works exactly as well as "we put our name on this software."

### E2 — The Builders `[PROPOSED]`

> *"We build, then we build on it."*

YBMA as a builder of things — software today, buildings and value chains and financial products
later, businesses always. Compounding: each thing built becomes the ground for the next.

- **Communicates:** productivity, patience, substance over talk, long-term construction.
- **Strengths:** genuinely sector-agnostic — software, property, agriculture and financial
  products are all built things. Naturally African in register without a single motif: building
  is the honest story of this generation on the continent. Energetic and youthful without being
  juvenile.
- **Weaknesses:** "we're builders" is common startup language and near-invisible in technology
  circles. The literal reading risks pinning you to construction once a property division exists.
  Says nothing about *quality*, which is where your advantage probably lies.
- **Scalability:** high, with the construction-literalism caveat.

### E3 — New Ownership `[PROPOSED]`

> *"A new generation owns the infrastructure."*

YBMA as evidence that young Africans can own the systems their economies run on rather than being
employed by people who do.

- **Communicates:** ambition, generational change, ownership, pride.
- **Strengths:** the most emotionally charged of the five. Powerful for recruiting talent and
  attracting investors. Culturally African with no reliance on visual symbolism.
- **Weaknesses:** anchored to your youth, so it expires (see 0.3). Leans harder on African
  identity than your stated global-first ambition allows. Can read as political, which narrows
  your options with certain clients. And it is about *you*, not the customer, which makes it a
  poor foundation for commercial messaging.
- **Scalability:** sector-wise high, time-wise poor. Better as a founding story than an essence.

### E4 — Range `[REJECTED]`

> *"Capable across categories."*

Included so the reasoning is on record, because versatility is in your brief.

Range as an essence says: we can do many things. **Nobody buys range.** A customer with a problem
wants the firm that is unmistakably right for *that* problem. Range is also the exact signal a
cash-hungry, unfocused company sends, which makes it the opposite of the credibility you want.

**Verdict:** rejected as an essence. Preserved as a structural principle in section 7.

### E5 — Durability `[PROPOSED]`

> *"Made to still be working later."*

YBMA as the antidote to disposability: systems still running in five years, buildings still
standing well, decisions that hold. Explicitly anti-trend.

- **Communicates:** quality, patience, engineering seriousness, low drama.
- **Strengths:** provable and testable, unlike most brand claims. Transfers beautifully across
  sectors — durability is the shared language of software, property and agriculture. Protects you
  from trend-chasing by making it a brand violation. And it quietly attacks a real pattern in
  project services: things get built and then abandoned.
- **Weaknesses:** cold and slightly conservative — low on the energy you want. Durability takes
  years to demonstrate, and you do not have years of track record yet. Slightly at odds with the
  experimental streak in your brief.
- **Scalability:** very high, and it ages in your favour rather than against you.

### Recommendation `[PROPOSED]`

**E1 (The Signature) as the essence — with E5 (Durability) as its proof.**

E1 is the only one of the five built on something you already have and nobody else can take. Your
name is currently your biggest brand liability: four letters with no meaning, no image and no
story. Making the name *mean accountability* converts the liability into the core asset in one
move, and it works at every scale — a ₦300,000 website and a future property division both
improve when a named person is answerable.

E5 supplies what E1 lacks. "Our names are on it" is a promise about people; "it still works in
five years" is the evidence that the promise is real. Together they form a closed loop:
**accountability is the claim, durability is the receipt.**

Candidate essence lines, none chosen:
- *"Our name is the warranty."*
- *"Signed work."*
- *"Built to a standard."*

The essence is an internal instrument. It does not have to become public copy.

## 2. Brand positioning

Positioning is the space you occupy in a buyer's head relative to the alternatives.

### P1 — The Accountable Partner `[PROPOSED — RECOMMENDED as commercial position]`

> *"Work you can put a name to."*

**What YBMA stands for:** the firm that does not go quiet. Named ownership, visible progress, bad
news early, and a stated commitment to finish.

- **Why interesting:** it competes on the axis most project-services buyers are actually anxious
  about, rather than the axis suppliers like to talk about. Nobody chooses a developer because
  they wanted more innovation; they choose one because they are afraid of being left with a
  half-built thing.
- **Why it works:** deliverable by a four-person firm on day one, with no capital. Reinforced
  rather than diluted by founder involvement. And it is a position larger competitors
  structurally struggle to copy, because at scale partners sell and juniors deliver.
- **Weaknesses:** it is a delivery promise, so it lives or dies operationally. It sets a low
  ceiling on glamour — it will never be the exciting brand in the room. And if you cannot keep
  it, it is more damaging than a vague position would have been.
- **Differentiation:** potentially strong, dependent on how rare this actually is in your market.
  `[INFERENCE]` I believe abandonment and silence are common complaints in Nigerian project
  services, but I have no data and you should not let me pretend otherwise.
- **Scalability:** excellent. Trust deficits in Nigerian real estate and financial services are,
  if anything, larger than in software. This position gets *more* valuable as you diversify.
- **Risks:** requires you to say no to work you cannot deliver. That is the expensive part.

### P2 — The Operating Partner `[PROPOSED — park and revisit]`

> *"We build and run the systems you grow on."*

**What YBMA stands for:** not a vendor hired for a project, but an operator that stays and runs
things. Ongoing responsibility over one-off delivery.

- **Why interesting:** shifts you from project revenue to recurring revenue, which is the single
  biggest determinant of whether the group ambition is fundable.
- **Why it works:** scales conceptually into every target sector — real estate is operating
  physical assets, agriculture is operating a value chain, finance is operating capital.
- **Weaknesses:** abstract and hard to sell to an individual buying a website. Probably
  premature — operating is a capability claim, and you would be claiming it before proving it.
- **Differentiation:** moderate. Managed-services positioning is well populated globally.
- **Scalability:** excellent, arguably the most scalable of the four.
- **Risks:** over-promising capacity you do not have. Revisit in 18–24 months.

### P3 — Institution Builders `[PROPOSED — RECOMMENDED as vision narrative]`

> *"Building institutions for the next African century."*

**What YBMA stands for:** the long game. Not a company chasing contracts but a group assembling
durable institutions.

- **Why interesting:** gives you a fifteen-year runway in one sentence and makes the
  parent-company plan legible to talent and investors rather than looking like overreach.
- **Why it works:** attracts exactly the people and capital a group needs. Impossible to outgrow.
- **Weaknesses:** enormous credibility gap. From four people selling websites it reads as
  grandiose, and grandiosity is the fastest way to lose a serious buyer. It also says nothing to
  a customer with a problem today.
- **Differentiation:** low as stated — ambitious narrative is cheap and widely used.
- **Scalability:** total, but it is a vision rather than a position.
- **Risks:** becoming a company that is better at narrating itself than at delivering.

### P4 — The Standard `[PROPOSED — as internal bar, not external messaging]`

> *"World-class, from here."*

**What YBMA stands for:** refusing the local discount. The same standard of work a client would
get from London or Singapore, delivered from Nigeria.

- **Why interesting:** directly addresses your global-first ambition and gives the African
  identity question a confident answer instead of a defensive one.
- **Why it works:** genuinely motivating internally — it is a standard you can hold each other to.
  Transfers cleanly to every sector.
- **Weaknesses:** "world-class" is one of the most devalued phrases in business writing. It can
  read as contempt for local peers, which is costly in a relationship-driven market. And quality
  claims are table stakes — every competitor says it.
- **Differentiation:** low as a claim, high as a practice. The differentiation is in the evidence,
  not the words.
- **Scalability:** high.
- **Risks:** sounding like an aspiration rather than a description.

### Comparison

| Territory | Sellable today | Ownable | Scales beyond tech | Ages well | Main risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **P1 Accountable Partner** | High | High | High | High | Operational discipline |
| **P2 Operating Partner** | Low | Medium | High | High | Premature capability claim |
| **P3 Institution Builders** | Very low | Low | Total | High | Grandiosity |
| **P4 The Standard** | Medium | Low | High | Medium | Devalued language |

### Recommendation `[PROPOSED]`

**Run P1 as the commercial position and P3 as the vision narrative, with P1 laddering into P3.**

These are two different artefacts for two different audiences, and collapsing them into one
sentence is a common and expensive mistake.

- **P1** is what a buyer hears. It drives the website, proposals and sales conversations.
- **P3** is what a recruit, partner or investor hears. It lives in the about page, internal
  language and the five-year plan.

They connect honestly: **institutions are just accumulated kept promises.** That laddering means
every accountable project you deliver is literal evidence for the larger claim, so the vision
earns itself instead of being asserted.

Fold **P4** in as an internal quality bar rather than external messaging — hold the standard, do
not announce it. Park **P2** and revisit once there is recurring revenue to point at.

## 3. Brand promise

A promise is a commitment a customer could hold you to. **If it cannot be broken, it is not a
promise — it is decoration.** That single test eliminates most of what companies write here.

### B1 `[PROPOSED]` — "We finish what we start."

Concrete, breakable, and it addresses a real fear rather than a marketing theme. Works in any
sector.
**Limitation:** it is a floor, not a ceiling — it promises competence rather than excellence, and
a sophisticated buyer may hear it as a low bar.

### B2 `[PROPOSED]` — "You will always know exactly where your project stands."

A transparency promise. Easy to keep, easy to verify, immediately felt, and unusually
differentiating because most firms manage clients by reassurance rather than by information.
**Limitation:** narrow. It is about process, not outcome, and it does not stretch to a group brand.

### B3 `[PROPOSED — RECOMMENDED]` — "Whatever we put our name on, we stand behind."

A warranty promise, and the direct expression of the recommended essence. Works identically for a
website, a building and a subsidiary, which makes it the only one of these that functions as a
*group* promise.
**Limitation:** it needs the proof mechanisms below, or it is just a sentiment.

### B4 `[PROPOSED]` — "We build things that still work in five years."

A durability promise. Specific, falsifiable, and anti-trend by construction.
**Limitation:** unverifiable for five years, and it emphasises the artefact over the relationship.

### The strategic difference between them

- **B1 and B2** are promises about *process* — cheap to keep, immediately credible, limited upside.
- **B4** is a promise about the *product* — strong, but slow to prove.
- **B3** is a promise about *the company itself*, which is the only kind that survives
  diversification, because a holding company does not have a product.

### Recommendation `[PROPOSED]`

**B3 as the master-brand promise, with B1, B2 and B4 as its operating proof.**

What makes B3 meaningful rather than pleasant is that it is enforceable. It should be enforced
through four mechanisms decided now, before any design work:

1. **A named owner on every engagement** — one of the four, by name, in the contract.
2. **Status the client can see without asking.** The moment a client has to chase you for an
   update, the promise is already broken.
3. **Bad news first, and from the owner.** Delay disclosed the day it is known, not the week it
   becomes obvious.
4. **A stated support period after delivery**, written down and priced in, so "we stand behind it"
   has a date attached.

**Those four are the brand.** Everything designed later is a way of signalling that they exist.

## 4. Brand personality

Traits are worthless as adjectives. Each primary trait below is expressed as behaviour — what it
forces you to do, and what it forbids.

### Primary traits `[PROPOSED]`

**1. Accountable**
- *Behaviour:* names attached to work; mistakes announced before they are discovered; no passive
  voice about failure.
- *Communication:* "I missed this" rather than "there was a delay."
- *Delivery:* a single owner per engagement, never a team address.
- *Visual:* signatures, individual names, real photographs of real people — not stock imagery of
  nobody.

**2. Direct**
- *Behaviour:* answers the question that was asked. Quotes a number when asked for a number.
- *Communication:* short sentences, no jargon, no "leverage" or "solutions"; explains trade-offs
  rather than selling.
- *Decisions:* says no out loud instead of going quiet.
- *Visual:* high clarity, minimal ornament, information over atmosphere.

**3. Exacting**
- *Behaviour:* finishes the last ten percent that clients rarely see and always feel.
- *Delivery:* will not ship something it would be embarrassed to be shown in two years.
- *Decisions:* reduces scope rather than lowering quality.
- *Visual:* tight geometry, disciplined spacing, alignment that holds up under a ruler.

**4. Composed**
- *Behaviour:* does not panic in a crisis or oversell in a pitch.
- *Communication:* even register whether the news is good or bad; no exclamation marks.
- *Customers:* absorbs a client's stress rather than amplifying it.
- *Visual:* quiet, spacious, low contrast in tone even when bold in form.

**5. Ambitious**
- *Behaviour:* reinvests; takes on work slightly beyond current comfort; thinks in decades.
- *Communication:* states direction, never inflates achievement. Says "building" where a weaker
  brand would say "leading."
- *Decisions:* weighs the ten-year consequence, not the quarter.

**6. Curious**
- *Behaviour:* tries new tools and methods constantly — *on its own time and its own projects*.
- *Delivery:* the client gets the proven approach; the experiment happens internally first.
- This is the whole resolution of the experimental-vs-dependable tension: **curiosity inward,
  reliability outward.**

**7. Generous**
- *Behaviour:* explains rather than mystifies; gives away useful knowledge; shares credit publicly.
- *Customers:* tells a client when they do not need what they asked for.
- *Visual:* approachable, legible, never gatekeeping.

### Secondary traits `[PROPOSED]`

- **Warm** — human, remembers people, not coldly corporate.
- **Dry-witted** — light touch, used sparingly; never the point.
- **Resourceful** — works well within constraint rather than complaining about it.
- **Locally fluent** — understands power cuts, procurement cycles, cash-flow reality and how
  decisions actually get made.

### Traits to avoid deliberately `[PROPOSED]`

| Avoid | Because |
|---|---|
| **Hype-y** | Excitement is the cheapest signal in technology and the first thing serious buyers discount. |
| **Boastful** | You have little to boast about yet, and claiming otherwise is detectable. |
| **Servile** | The classic agency failure — agreeing to everything, which destroys the authority you need to be trusted with big work. Saying no is a trust-building act. |
| **Trend-chasing** | Directly contradicts durability, and dates the brand. |
| **Corporate-bland** | The default failure mode when a young company tries to look serious. Seriousness comes from precision, not from beige. |
| **Preachy about Africa** | Turns your origin into a lecture. See section 6. |
| **Aloof** | Apple can be mysterious; it is famous. Mystery from an unknown company reads as evasion. |
| **Cute** | Undermines every trust signal you are trying to build. |

## 5. Brand archetype

A caution before the answer: archetypes are a decision-making shortcut, not a requirement. They
are useful when a team of four has to make consistent calls without meeting about every one. They
are harmful when they become a costume. **This is a low-stakes decision compared with positioning
and architecture**, and I would not spend much time on it.

| Archetype | Fit | Assessment |
|---|---|---|
| **Creator** | Strong | Craft, making, exacting standards, pride in the artefact. Matches the builder and durability energy, and matches what you actually do all day. |
| **Ruler** | Strong | Order, stewardship, institutional ambition, premium credibility. This is the holding-company archetype — both Samsung and Dangote are Ruler-dominant. |
| **Hero** | Poor | Implies rescue and struggle. Massively overused in tech, and it centres you rather than the client. |
| **Explorer** | Poor | Fits the experimental streak but actively undermines the trust you have not yet earned. Explorers are exciting and not reassuring. |
| **Sage** | Weak | Requires authority you do not have yet; leads to a lecturing tone. |

### Recommendation `[PROPOSED]`

**Creator primary, Ruler secondary — shifting toward Ruler over time.**

Creator is right for now because it is honest: you are four people who make things, and the craft
is where your credibility will come from. Ruler is the direction of travel, because a group that
stewards multiple businesses is inherently a Ruler brand. Expect the ratio to invert somewhere
around the second real division.

Practically, this combination tells you what to do when you are unsure: **choose the more crafted
option over the more clever one, and the more orderly option over the more exciting one.** That is
the entire value of the exercise.

## 6. Global identity, African origin

This is the most interesting problem in the brief, and there is an answer that does not require
compromise.

### The principle `[PROPOSED]`

> **African by authorship. Global by standard.**

YBMA does not need to *look* African, because it *is* African. The authorship is a fact, and facts
do not need illustrating.

What people call "African branding" is usually a company compensating for an anxiety about being
taken seriously — either over-decorating to claim identity, or scrubbing every trace to pass as
foreign. Both are defensive. **Confidence is stating your origin once, plainly, and then letting
the work be judged by a global standard with no allowances asked for.**

Flutterwave is the proof this works. It is unmistakably African-founded, says so directly, and has
never needed a pattern to establish it. The craft is global; the origin is a stated fact, not a
theme.

### A structural argument against motifs `[INFERENCE]`

There is a reason beyond taste. Look at your own names: Yusuf, Benard, Michael, Assad. That set
reads as multi-ethnic and multi-faith — plausibly spanning northern and southern Nigeria, Muslim
and Christian.

If that is right, it is strategically significant. **Any single-culture visual language — a Yoruba
motif, a northern pattern, an Igbo reference — would misrepresent your own founding group, and
would quietly exclude some of you.** YBMA's composition is itself pan-Nigerian. The neutral,
craft-led route is not merely more tasteful; it is the only one that is *accurate*.

That is a much stronger argument than aesthetic preference, and it is worth saying out loud when
someone inevitably suggests adding a pattern.

*(Correct me if the inference about your backgrounds is wrong — it does not change the
recommendation, but it does change how strong the argument is.)*

### Where culture can legitimately live `[PROPOSED]`

1. **Behaviour, not decoration.** The most authentically African thing about YBMA will be how it
   acts: depth of relationship over transaction, real hospitality, responsiveness, respect for
   seniority in B2B settings, showing up in person when it matters, an orientation toward the
   people around the business and not only the business. None of this appears on a moodboard, and
   all of it is felt by every client.
2. **Language and rhythm.** Nigerian English has a directness and a humour that most corporate
   copy strips out. Writing that sounds like a person from Lagos speaking carefully — rather than
   a translation of an American template — is a far more distinctive cultural signature than any
   graphic device, and competitors cannot copy it.
3. **Naming.** If internal programmes, products or divisions are ever named in a Nigerian
   language, they should be named because the word *means the right thing*, never as flavouring.
   Given the multi-ethnic point above, this needs care.
4. **Light and material, abstracted.** Colour can legitimately come from the lived environment —
   the specific quality of harmattan light, laterite, brass, indigo — provided it is abstracted
   into a considered palette rather than illustrated. **Real risk:** terracotta-and-ochre is now
   itself a global design cliché, so this route is narrower than it looks. It belongs to the
   colour stage, not here.
5. **Ambition as the statement.** Building something to a global standard from Nigeria *is* the
   cultural argument. The confidence is the content.

### Explicitly out of bounds `[REJECTED]`

Adinkra and kente (Ghanaian, and not yours) · mudcloth · masks · acacia silhouettes · sunset
gradients · the outline of the continent · the green-gold-red triad · drums · "Ubuntu" language
(southern African) · "Africa rising" rhetoric · anything that would let a viewer guess the
continent before they knew the company.

### One honest counter-argument

Going fully geography-neutral has a cost, and it is the outcome you said you did not want: a brand
that is culturally empty. It is also a losing tactic — concealing origin reads as embarrassment,
and it is discoverable in about four seconds anyway.

The recommendation is therefore **asymmetric on purpose: state the origin plainly and often in
words, and never in ornament.** The website says Lagos. The logo does not.

## 7. YBMA as a future parent company

**This section constrains the logo more than any other**, so it is worth getting right before
anything is drawn.

```
YBMA
├── YBMA Technology      software, web, IT consulting    ← today
├── YBMA Properties      real estate
├── YBMA Agriculture     agriculture
├── YBMA Capital         financial services
└── YBMA Ventures        future sectors
```

### The four models

| Model | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|
| **Branded house**<br>*YBMA Technology, YBMA Properties* | Every division's success compounds into one reputation. Cheapest possible option — one identity, one set of assets, one story. New divisions launch with borrowed trust on day one. It is the Samsung and Dangote model, and it fits a group with no marketing budget. | Contagion: one failure damages everything. Constrains divisions whose audiences need a different register — a consumer app may need warmth the parent brand does not have. Harder to sell a division later. Requires the master brand to be broad enough to stretch. |
| **Endorsed brands**<br>*"Kanari, a YBMA company"* | Division-level freedom plus borrowed credibility. Natural fit for acquisitions and consumer ventures. Risk is partly contained. Easier to divest. | Each division needs its own brand investment. The master brand accumulates equity much more slowly. More assets to maintain and easier to become inconsistent. |
| **House of brands**<br>*independent names; YBMA invisible* | Maximum flexibility and total risk isolation. Each brand perfectly targeted. | YBMA itself builds no equity whatsoever, which contradicts your stated wish for YBMA to be known. Expensive — it is the Unilever model and it needs Unilever's budget. `[REJECTED for now]` |
| **Hybrid** | Branded house for core B2B divisions; endorsement for acquired, consumer or higher-risk ventures. Most large groups end up here. | Needs governance rules to stop it becoming ad hoc. Premature to design in detail now. |

### Recommendation `[PROPOSED]`

**Branded house now, engineered to flex into a hybrid later.**

The reasoning is partly that your name is meaningless — and here that is an advantage. "YBMA"
carries no sector baggage, so *YBMA Agriculture* is no stranger than *YBMA Technology*. A name like
"CodeCraft" could never do this. **Your name is badly suited to consumer recall and unusually well
suited to a diversified group, and the architecture is where that pays off.**

Two hard design constraints follow, and they go straight into the eventual logo brief:

1. **The master brand carries no sector signifiers.** Nothing about technology in the mark, ever.
   The sector lives in the division word. This alone rules out most of what a tech-company brief
   would produce.
2. **The identity must work at two very different sizes of role** — as a primary lockup
   (*YBMA Technology*) and as a small endorsement stamp (*a YBMA company*), in one line of small
   type, in one colour. Designing for the second case from the start is what keeps the hybrid
   option open.

### One structural recommendation for now, not later `[PROPOSED]`

**Start trading the current business as YBMA Technology immediately**, at four people, before
there is anything else.

It costs nothing and it prevents the single most likely failure of this whole plan: that YBMA
becomes permanently synonymous with the company that builds websites, and the group idea stays a
slide. **The separation has to exist from the first business card, or it will never exist at all.**

## 8. Potential differentiation

**Everything in this section is `[HYPOTHESIS]`.** No research has been done, no clients have been
spoken to, and there is no data on your market. Anyone who tells you they have identified a market
gap for you in one session is selling you something. This is where I would look.

| Opportunity | Why it could hold | What would have to be true |
|---|---|---|
| **Named accountability** | Structurally unownable by anyone else, because it is your names. Free to start. Compounds with every delivered project. | That buyers in your market feel real anxiety about supplier reliability, and would pay for its removal. |
| **Radical transparency** | Small teams can do this easily; large ones find it politically difficult. A rare case where your size is the advantage. | That clients actually want visibility rather than reassurance. Some prefer not to know. |
| **Founder-level attention** | Four owners means senior involvement as standard, against the industry pattern where partners sell and juniors deliver. | **Honest flag:** this stops being true past roughly 20–30 people, so building the brand on it creates a trap. Better as a current proof point than a permanent position. |
| **Durability and aftercare** | Attacks the build-and-abandon pattern directly, and creates recurring revenue while doing it. | That buyers will pay for maintenance rather than treating it as a warranty they are owed. |
| **Cross-sector operating literacy** | Long-term: a group that has actually operated in property and agriculture understands its clients' businesses differently from a pure software firm. | Years away. Unavailable as a claim today. |

### What to do instead of guessing

**Eight to twelve conversations, thirty minutes each**, with people who have bought this kind of
work — ideally including clients who chose someone else. Three questions:

1. What went wrong last time?
2. What were you afraid of before signing?
3. What actually made you choose?

That is a week of work, it costs nothing, and it is the difference between a positioning that is
true and one that is merely plausible.

## 9. The YBMA person

One challenge before the description. Your brief says the personality is a young African founder.
**If that person is twenty-four, the brand cannot win a state government contract or an enterprise
IT engagement** — and those are on your target list. I have aged them deliberately.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Age** | **Around 34.** Old enough to have shipped, failed, and learned what that costs. Young enough to still be hungry and to work faster than the incumbents. Not 24 — that reads as unproven. Not 55 — that reads as the firms you are trying to beat. |
| **Personality** | Calm, precise, quietly competitive. Listens more than they talk, then says the one thing that reframes the conversation. Comfortable with silence. Does not need to win the meeting. |
| **Appearance** | Well-made plain clothes, no visible logos. Good shoes, kept clean. A decent watch, not a loud one. Carries a notebook and actually writes in it. Looks like they could be the most junior or the most senior person in the room, and it takes ten minutes to find out which. |
| **Communication** | Short sentences. Concrete nouns. Answers the question that was asked and then stops. Gives bad news first and without cushioning. Writes clearly enough that their emails get forwarded. Never uses a word to sound clever, and never says "leverage," "solutions" or "seamless." |
| **Behaviour** | Arrives prepared, having read the material. Follows up unprompted. Remembers what you said last time. Does the unglamorous last ten percent. Says no to about a third of what they are offered. |
| **Decisions** | Slower than people expect, then fully committed. Asks what this looks like in ten years. Will trade short-term revenue for reputation without agonising, because they think reputation *is* the asset. |
| **With customers** | Gives a small client the same rigour as a large one, and is honest about what the budget buys instead of pretending it buys more. Tells a client when they do not need what they came to ask for. Never flatters. |
| **Believes** | That standards travel — the bar does not drop because of where you are. That reputation compounds and nothing else does. That nothing is beneath doing properly. That the work should outlive the contract. |
| **Motivated by** | Building something that is still standing when their children are adults, and proving the standard can be set from here rather than imported. |
| **Would never** | Overpromise to win a deal. Blame the client. Go quiet when a project is in trouble. Copy a competitor. Discount the standard to keep a price. Use a buzzword to seem sophisticated. Take work they know they cannot do well. |

## 10. Strategic tensions

These are the contradictions in the brief. They cannot be eliminated, only managed — and a brand
is largely the accumulated record of how a company chose to resolve them.

### Youthful vs. professional
**Conflict:** too young and you lose enterprise and government work. Too professional and you
become the beige firm you are trying to displace, with none of its track record.
**→ Resolution:** *Young in tempo, mature in craft.* Youth shows up as speed, curiosity, appetite
and responsiveness. It never shows up in the aesthetic or in informality. The visual identity
should be indistinguishable from that of a thirty-year-old firm; the behaviour should be
unmistakably faster.

### African vs. global
**Conflict:** lean African and you get filed under regional. Lean global and you become placeless,
which is the culturally empty outcome you rejected — and it also reads as concealment.
**→ Resolution:** *African by authorship, global by standard.* Origin in the words, never in the
ornament. See section 6.

### Innovative vs. trustworthy
**Conflict:** innovation signals risk. Buyers of infrastructure are buying the absence of risk.
Meanwhile a brand with no inventiveness cannot attract good engineers.
**→ Resolution:** *Inventive in method, conventional in promise.* Experiment internally; deliver
proven approaches externally. The client experiences the innovation as unusual speed and quality,
never as being someone's experiment.

### Ambitious vs. credible
**Conflict:** stated ambition attracts talent and capital. Overstated ambition from four people
destroys credibility instantly, and it is easily checked.
**→ Resolution:** State ambition as direction, never as achievement. "Building" not "leading."
Never claim a scale you do not have; never apologise for the scale you do. Put the ambition where
it belongs — recruiting and investors — and keep it out of the sales conversation.

### Versatile vs. focused
**Conflict:** the group needs breadth. Every individual buyer wants a specialist. "We do
everything" is the signature phrase of a firm that does nothing especially well.
**→ Resolution:** Versatility lives in the architecture, focus lives in the offer. The group is
broad; the division the client meets is narrow. **Never publish a services list that spans four
sectors on one page.**

### Technology today vs. diversified tomorrow
**Conflict:** you need to be credible as a technology firm now, and unencumbered by technology
later.
**→ Resolution:** Sector credibility lives entirely in the division name and its content. The
master brand stays clean. This is the reason for both the "no tech signifiers" rule and the
recommendation to start trading as YBMA Technology now.

### Experimental vs. dependable
**Conflict:** the same conflict as innovation, but about temperament rather than output.
Experimental cultures break commitments; dependable cultures stop learning.
**→ Resolution:** Give experimentation a named internal home and a budget, so it has somewhere to
go that is not a client's project. Then dependability externally is a policy rather than a
personality struggle.

### Premium vs. accessible
**Conflict:** the hardest one in practice, and the one most likely to break. Early-stage firms take
cheap work to survive, then reduce quality to make the margin, and within a year the premium
positioning is a claim nobody internally believes.
**→ Resolution:** Premium in craft, accessible in manner, transparent in price. Take the small
work — then **scope down instead of discounting**. A small budget buys less, never worse. Price
varies by division and by scope; the standard does not vary at all. **Expect to be tested on this
within six months.**

### The ones that matter

**Accountable** is the load-bearing word. If only one survives, keep this one — it is the essence,
the promise and the differentiator in a single adjective.

**Exacting** does a job that "quality" and "excellence" cannot: it implies a specific standard
being applied by someone with judgement, and it carries a slight edge. *Excellence is a claim;
exacting is a behaviour.*

**Composed** is how you get credibility without age. Young firms signal youth by being energetic
in the wrong register — over-enthusiastic email, exclamation marks, urgency. **Calm is the
cheapest maturity available.**

**"Innovative" is on the reject list on purpose**, even though it is in your brief. It is the
single most-claimed adjective in technology, which makes it worth approximately nothing, and it
pulls you toward the visual clichés you already rejected. You do not need the word: *inventive in
method, conventional in promise* is both more accurate and more useful.

**"Vibrant"** is on the list because it is the euphemism that leads directly to an
ochre-and-terracotta palette and a pattern library. It is how African-cliché branding gets
approved — nobody asks for a cliché, they ask for vibrancy.

**"Youthful" is deliberately absent from both lists.** It is a real and valuable internal energy,
and a liability as a brand keyword, because it invites immature expression and it expires. Keep it
in how you work. Keep it out of the brief.

## 12. Decisions required from the founders

Three of these block useful further work. Four are important but not blocking. The list is short
on purpose — these are only the questions whose answers would change the strategy itself.

### BLOCKING

#### Q1 — How is "YBMA" said out loud? `[DECISION NEEDED]`

Spelled as letters — *why-bee-em-ay*, four syllables — or said as a word, *YIB-ma* or *YUB-ma*,
two syllables?

**This is the highest-leverage unanswered question in the project**, and it changes the logo, the
verbal identity, the recall strategy and every line of copy.

The precedents are instructive. IKEA is founder initials plus place names, said as a word, and it
became one of the most memorable brand names on earth. KPMG is said as letters, works fine, and
took decades inside a narrow professional-services market where the audience was small and repeat.
Four-letter names said as letters are hard work for recall; two-syllable words are easy.

Say both aloud twenty times, in English and in your own languages, and decide which one you would
rather hear a client use on the phone. **Then never vary it.**

#### Q2 — Is the group ambition funded, or aspirational? `[DECISION NEEDED]`

What is the actual three-year plan, and where does the capital for a second division come from —
retained profit, external investment, partnership?

This is not a judgement on the ambition. Branding an operating services firm and branding a
genuine holding group are different jobs with different budgets and different outputs. If the
honest answer is "aspiration for now," that is a perfectly good answer — it means we build a
technology brand under a clean, extensible parent and spend nothing on group-level apparatus until
there is a group.

#### Q3 — Who actually pays the bills over the next eighteen months? `[DECISION NEEDED]`

One primary audience. Not a list.

The master brand can stay broad; the go-to-market cannot. An individual buying a website, an SME,
and a government ministry require different proof, different price architecture, different sales
motion and different visual register. Pick the one that most plausibly funds the next eighteen
months, and the rest is designed outward from there.

### IMPORTANT

#### Q4 — Do all four of you want the same thing? `[DECISION NEEDED]`

Asked plainly: does each of you want to build a diversified group, or would one or two of you be
happy with a profitable services firm? Both are legitimate, and they produce different brands.
Multi-founder brands break here more often than anywhere else, and it is much cheaper to find out
now than in year four.

#### Q5 — What happens to the name if a founder leaves? `[DECISION NEEDED]`

The name is made of four specific people. If one exits in year six, does YBMA keep the name? Is
that written down anywhere? This is a real risk to an asset you are about to invest heavily in,
and it is a paperwork problem today and a crisis later.

#### Q6 — Where does expansion go first? `[DECISION NEEDED]`

Nigeria-deep, Africa-wide, or international and diaspora? This sets the craft bar and the tone.
Africa-wide raises hard naming and language questions early; international raises the quality bar
and changes nothing else.

#### Q7 — What already exists that should be known about? `[DECISION NEEDED]`

Where is YBMA LTD incorporated — the "LTD" suffix raises Nigeria versus UK, and the answer changes
the global-first story. Which domains do you own? Is there any current look, however provisional,
that existing clients already recognise? Any trademark searches done?
