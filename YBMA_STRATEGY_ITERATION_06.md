# Executive Summary

**Round 2 re-interviewed the same five Kaduna businesses as Round 1, using the new behavioural
guide.** Same five sectors, same city, staff counts now recorded (3–7). This is a **longitudinal
deepening, not the Round 2 that was designed** — none of the thirteen designed profiles were
added, no Lagos or Abuja, no professional services, no satisfied large spender, no supply-side
informant. That matters, and section *Consolidated Customer Evidence* explains exactly what it
means for each conclusion.

**But the new guide worked.** Round 1 produced adjectives; Round 2 produced numbers — actual prices
paid, competing quotes, discretionary-spend ceilings, monthly software spend, quantified loss
incidents, a full price ladder and four trade-offs from all five respondents. The instrument is
validated.

### Six findings, in order of strength

**1. The single most consistent finding across both rounds: these businesses can see revenue and
cannot see margin.** `[EVIDENCE]` All five described the same experience unprompted — sales looked
good, actual profit was much smaller than believed. All five named a *profit* number, not a revenue
number, as the one thing they would want every morning. All five had already tried to fix it. Two
had paid. **This is the only hypothesis that clears its pre-registered threshold, and it clears
every clause of it.**

**2. The ₦500,000–₦2,000,000 price band is not supported for this segment.** `[EVIDENCE]` Asked
what solving their own named problem would be worth, the five self-valued at **₦100,000–₦300,000**.
At ₦500,000, four of five rejected or strongly resisted. **At ₦1,000,000, none accepted.** At
₦2,000,000, all five refused. Actual technology spend to date: ₦150,000 and ₦350,000. The
pre-registered threshold from Session 5 fails on two of its three clauses and triggers its
SERIOUS CONCERN condition.

**3. The threshold's second requirement produced the most valuable output of the session.** Session
5 required recording *what distinguishes respondents above the price wall from those below it.* The
one respondent closest to clearing ₦500k is the only one that **already pays monthly for business
software**, has the **highest transaction volume**, has **already spent the most on technology**,
has **four staff using a shared system**, and is the **only one showing urgency**. That is not
"1–10 staff MSMEs." `[HYPOTHESIS]` **The real target may be: businesses that already pay for
software and have outgrown it.**

**4. The same line resolves three separate questions at once.** `[INTERPRETATION]` Prior purchase
predicts price tolerance, willingness to pay for ownership, *and* dependency literacy —
simultaneously. The two respondents who have bought technology are the two who pay double for
control; the three who have not are the three who will not. This is the strongest structural
insight available from the data.

**5. Solutions in this market fail on the input, not the output.** `[EVIDENCE]` Four of five
abandonments were data-entry or adoption failures — *"too much work to enter everything,"*
*"nobody was entering information properly,"* *"five minutes entering information after every
trip."* Not one abandonment was about missing features. **Every vendor promises features; the
actual failure mode is that nobody can sustain feeding the system.** No competitor positions
against this.

**6. Two prior recommendations are now falsified by their own pre-registered thresholds.**
"Built to do more" / capacity: **DROP** — zero of five connected a growth plan to a systems need
unprompted, and three of five explicitly deferred. "Built properly" / standards: **RETIRE as
customer-facing** — zero quality or rigour language across ten interviews.

### The recommendation

**YBMA is not ready to choose a final positioning — but the reason has changed, and the new reason
is better.**

The blocker is no longer *"we do not know what customers want."* We now know, with unusual
consistency, what this segment's problem is and in what words. The blocker is that **the segment we
have researched cannot pay the price the business model requires.** That is a commercial question,
not a brand question, and it has to be answered first — because its answer determines whether the
brand is being built for these five businesses or for a materially different customer.

Leading direction: **"The correct figures" / margin truth** — their language, strongest evidence,
and it scales to the parent brand better than anything examined in six sessions. Held as the
leading hypothesis, not locked.

**Single most important next test: a real priced sales conversation, not a third interview round.**

## Evidence map by theme

### THEME 1 — Margin blindness *(the strongest finding in the study)*

**ROUND 1 EVIDENCE.** R5: *"You realize the profit isn't what you thought."* R1: stock the biggest
issue, unaware items had run out. R4: *"Everything is in my head and my book."* R2: managing
properly, prices changing. R3: managing the money. **5 of 5 mentioned; 3 of 5 named it their
primary constraint.**

**ROUND 2 EVIDENCE.** All five, unprompted in Section 1, and all five quantified a loss:

| Respondent | The problem, their words | Loss incident |
|---|---|---|
| Phone shop | *"Wrong stock figures and sometimes staff selling something without properly recording it"* | **₦45,000** |
| Foodstuff | *"Sometimes the physical quantity doesn't match what we think we have"* | **₦60,000** |
| Transport | *"Checking what each driver actually made and what was spent"* | **₦80,000–₦100,000** |
| Tailoring | *"Knowing exactly which stage each person's clothes are at"* | **₦25,000** |
| Mini-mart | *"Reconciling sales, cash and stock"* | **₦70,000** |

And the same experience, described five separate ways:

> Phone: *"We thought we had more profit from accessories than we actually did… some of the margins
> were very small."*
> Foodstuff: *"You think you've made more money because you've sold plenty, then you realize the
> buying price has increased and the actual profit is small."*
> Transport: *"Sometimes the gross income looks good until you remove fuel and maintenance."*
> Tailoring: *"There are many orders but not enough cash because customers haven't completed
> payment."*
> Mini-mart: *"We discovered some products weren't making as much profit as we thought."*

**"One number you'd want every morning without doing any work":** yesterday's profit after
expenses · yesterday's profit · net income per vehicle · outstanding customer payments · actual
profit after expenses. **5 of 5 named a money-truth number. 4 of 5 specifically named profit or
net, not revenue.**

**"If you could hand one part of this business to someone tomorrow":** stock management · stock and
purchasing · managing the drivers and their daily figures · following up on orders · stock control.
**5 of 5 named a records or information task.**

**Have they tried to fix it? 5 of 5.** Excel (×3), a stock application (₦150,000 paid), a
bookkeeping app, a customer-order app, POS software (₦350,000 paid), an accounting app.
**Paid to fix it: 2 of 5.**

**CONSISTENCY** Total. The only theme present in all ten interviews with no contradicting case.
**STRENGTH** Highest in the study, and it strengthened markedly in Round 2 from stated problem to
quantified loss with attempted remedies.
**BEHAVIOURAL EVIDENCE** Strong — five attempted solutions, two paid purchases, one abandonment.
**CUSTOMER LANGUAGE** *"The correct figures" · "what's left" · "what's selling" · "reconcile" ·
"the profit isn't what you thought" · "it doesn't calculate everything automatically."*

**WHAT IT MEANS** `[INTERPRETATION]` These businesses have adequate *records* and no *arithmetic*.
They can see turnover and cannot see margin. The problem is not that information is missing — it is
sitting in a notebook and in WhatsApp — it is that nothing computes it. **That is far more specific
than "visibility."**

**WHAT IT DOES NOT MEAN** It does not mean they will pay ₦500k+ to solve it — four of five
self-valued it at ₦100,000–₦300,000. It does not mean it is their *binding* constraint: in Round 1,
two of five named capital and customer demand first. And it does not mean a reporting product is
the answer — two respondents have already bought reporting and found it *"limited"* or *"not
really what I expected."*

### THEME 2 — Data-entry burden *(the strongest new finding of Round 2)*

**ROUND 1 EVIDENCE.** Present but unrecognised. R4: *"If I have to sit down for one hour to learn
how to use it, I probably won't use it."* Round 1 read this as a *learning* problem.

**ROUND 2 EVIDENCE.** Round 2 shows it is a *feeding* problem. Asked what makes something "too
complicated":

| Respondent | Definition of "too complicated" | Reads as |
|---|---|---|
| Foodstuff | *"When you need to enter too many things before you can make one simple sale"* | **Entry burden** |
| Transport | *"If the driver has to spend five minutes entering information after every trip"* | **Entry burden, quantified** |
| Tailoring | *"If I have to enter the same information in many places"* | **Duplicate entry** |
| Phone shop | *"If I need somebody to come every time I want to change something"* | **Dependency** |
| Mini-mart | *"When staff need to call somebody every time they want to perform a normal task"* | **Dependency** |

**Why solutions were abandoned — 4 of 5 are input failures, not feature failures:**

> Tailoring: *"Too much work"* · and on not buying: *"It looked like too much work to enter
> everything."*
> Transport: *"Nobody was entering information properly."* · *"Getting the drivers to use it
> consistently."*
> Foodstuff: *"I wasn't using it consistently"* (Excel) · *"I wasn't using it"* (bookkeeping app).
> Phone shop: *"It became troublesome."*

**CONSISTENCY** High. Independent expression by all five.
**STRENGTH** High, and **almost entirely new** — Round 1 misread it as a learning-curve issue.
**BEHAVIOURAL EVIDENCE** Very strong: four actual abandonments and one non-purchase, all
attributable to input cost.
**CUSTOMER LANGUAGE** *"Too much work" · "nobody was entering information properly" · "enter the
same information in many places" · "five minutes after every trip."*

**WHAT IT MEANS** `[INTERPRETATION]` **The failure mode in this market is the input, not the
output.** Every one of these businesses wants a number out and cannot sustain putting data in — and
in the transport case it is not the owner who won't, it is the staff. Notably, **three of five
define complexity as entry cost and two define it as needing to call someone.** The second pair are
precisely the two who have bought technology. For buyers, **simplicity and ownership converge**:
both mean *"I can operate this without depending on you."*

**WHAT IT DOES NOT MEAN** It does not mean fewer features. The forced trade-off was unanimous for
"three things," but every stated reason was about doing the needed things *properly* — not about
minimalism. And it does not mean YBMA can solve it: eliminating data entry is technically hard and
may not be deliverable at this price.

### THEME 3 — Price and willingness to pay *(the commercially decisive theme)*

**ROUND 1 EVIDENCE.** Price cited by 4 of 5 as a selection or rejection factor. **No figures at
all** — the central gap of Round 1.

**ROUND 2 EVIDENCE.** Complete, from all five.

| Respondent | Staff | Self-valuation of their own problem | ₦500k | ₦1m | ₦2m | Max discretionary spend ever | Tech spend to date | Monthly software |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Phone shop | 4 | ₦200k–₦300k | *"That's expensive. I'd need to really see it working"* | *"Too much"* | *"No"* | ₦700,000 | **₦150,000** | None |
| Foodstuff | 5 | ₦100k | *"Too much for me"* | *"No"* | *"Definitely not"* | ₦500,000 | ₦0 | None |
| Transport | 3 | ₦100k–₦150k | *"I'd have to think seriously about it"* | *"Not currently"* | *"No"* | ₦400,000 | ₦0 | None |
| Tailoring | 6 | ₦100k | *"That's too much"* | *"No"* | *"Definitely not"* | ₦300,000 | ₦0 | None |
| **Mini-mart** | **7** | **₦300k** | ***"Possible, if it is really good"*** | *"I'd need to see the benefit clearly"* | *"Too high for us currently"* | ₦600,000 | **₦350,000** | **₦20k–₦30k** |

Competing quotes actually received: **₦200,000 and ₦300,000** (phone shop, chose ₦150,000);
**₦300,000–₦500,000** (mini-mart, chose ₦350,000).

**Applying the Session 5 pre-registered threshold, unchanged:**

> SUPPORTED if — 4+ have spent ₦300k+ discretionarily **AND** 3+ react to ₦1m as *"expensive but
> possible"* **AND** 2+ have actually spent ₦500k+ on technology.

| Clause | Result |
|---|---|
| 4+ spent ₦300k+ discretionarily | **PASS** — all five: ₦700k, ₦600k, ₦500k, ₦400k, ₦300k |
| 3+ react to ₦1m as "expensive but possible" | **FAIL — zero.** Best response: *"I'd need to see the benefit clearly"* |
| 2+ have spent ₦500k+ on technology | **FAIL — zero.** Highest is ₦350,000 |

> SERIOUS CONCERN if the wall sits below ₦500k for the clear majority.

**Wall below ₦500k for 4 of 5. SERIOUS CONCERN triggered.**

**CONSISTENCY** Total within this segment.
**STRENGTH** High — grounded in actual prices paid, actual quotes received and actual spend
ceilings, not in stated intentions.
**CUSTOMER LANGUAGE** *"I would need to be making much more money than I am now before considering
it"* (foodstuff) · *"I'd need a very big business before spending that"* (tailoring) · *"I'd need
proof that it would actually increase what I'm making"* (transport).

**WHAT IT MEANS** `[EVIDENCE→INTERPRETATION]` For these five businesses the ₦500k–₦2m band is out
of reach, and not marginally. Their own valuation of their own most painful problem sits at roughly
**one fifth to one half of the bottom of the band.** Note also the ceiling pattern: every
respondent's largest-ever discretionary purchase was **physical** — shop equipment, renovation,
sewing machines, a vehicle repair. `[INTERPRETATION]` Physical assets clear a spending bar that
software does not, plausibly because the benefit is visible: *"I could see the benefit directly"*
(phone shop, ₦700k on equipment); *"I could see exactly what I was getting"* (foodstuff, ₦500k on
renovation).

**WHAT IT DOES NOT MEAN** It does **not** mean no Nigerian MSME will pay ₦500k–₦2m. It means these
five will not — and these five are five Kaduna micro-retailers, precisely the profile Session 4
flagged as possibly the wrong segment. **The businesses designed into Round 2 to test this question
were not interviewed.** The finding is therefore a strong negative for one segment and silent on
the one that matters commercially.

### THEME 4 — Ownership, access and dependency

**ROUND 1 EVIDENCE.** 2 direct (phone shop strongly, transport via platforms), 1 indirect
(mini-mart), 2 absent (*"I've never really thought about it"*). One behavioural case: the phone shop
abandoned a paid system over developer dependency.

**ROUND 2 EVIDENCE — the trade-off is the decisive instrument.**

| Respondent | ₦600k fully control **vs** ₦300k provider-controlled | Has bought technology? |
|---|---|---|
| Phone shop | **₦600k fully control** | **Yes** — burned, abandoned |
| Mini-mart | **₦600k fully control** | **Yes** — in use |
| Foodstuff | ₦300k — *"I'd prefer to control it, but I wouldn't pay double just for that"* | No |
| Transport | ₦300k — *"if the provider is reliable"* | No |
| Tailoring | ₦300k — *"if the company is reliable"* | No |

**2 of 5 pay double for control. 3 of 5 will not. The split is exact: the two who have bought,
versus the three who have not.**

**And both refusals carry the same condition — *"if the provider is reliable."*** `[INTERPRETATION]`
They are not rejecting control. They are saying **reliability substitutes for control.** If you can
be trusted, ownership is unnecessary. That makes ownership and reliability *substitutes rather than
complements*, and it means YBMA can compete on either — but should not assume ownership is an
unconditional good.

**Literacy — the dependency is universal and invisible:**

> Phone shop: domain — *"I'm not sure."* Logins — *"I don't think so."* Hosting — *"the developer
> handled that."* **And: *"Did you ask? No. I didn't know I was supposed to ask."***
> Mini-mart: has POS logins; can export POS data; ownership explained *"not in detail"*; asked
> *"some questions, but not everything."*
> Foodstuff / Transport / Tailoring: all three would lose customer contacts and conversations if
> the phone were lost. Contacts *"mostly on the phone."* Foodstuff: *"Some are backed up, but I
> haven't actually tested it."*

**All five have business-critical data on a phone or in WhatsApp. Not one has tested a restore.**

**H22 — can ownership be made legible to a buyer who does not know to ask?** `[EVIDENCE]` Partial
answer, and it is encouraging. **Both buyers now say they would ask next time:**

> Phone shop: *"I'd make sure I have access to everything and understand exactly what I'm paying
> for."*
> Mini-mart: *"Ask more questions about support and data access before paying."*

**Ownership literacy is acquired through injury.** `[INTERPRETATION]` Which makes ownership a
**second-purchase criterion, not a first-purchase one.**

**Unprompted, at the close — both buyers named the same pair of sins:**

> Phone shop: *"Don't take people's money and disappear. And don't make something that the business
> owner cannot understand or access."*
> Mini-mart: *"Don't lock the business into something they can't leave. And don't disappear when
> there's a problem."*

**Applying the Session 5 threshold:**

> ELEVATE if 2+ have actually changed, rejected or paid more for a provider over access
> *(behaviour)* **OR** 3+ can quantify a lock-in cost.

One behavioural case — the phone shop, which changed provider over support and wrote off ₦150,000.
**It is the same business in both rounds, so it counts once, not twice.** The mini-mart's is stated
future intent, not enacted behaviour. **ELEVATE not met.**

> DROP if the trade-off goes to the cheaper option for the clear majority.

3 of 5 — **just short of the DROP boundary.**

**VERDICT: HOLD.** Neither elevate nor drop, which is exactly what a pre-registered threshold
should return when the evidence is genuinely split. The hold is itself informative: **strong among
buyers, absent among non-buyers, and substitutable by reliability.**

### THEME 5 — Support, continuity and abandonment

**ROUND 1 EVIDENCE.** 3 of 3 respondents with any technology experience raised it; neither of the
two without did. R5: *"Technology companies should not just collect money and disappear."*

**ROUND 2 EVIDENCE.** Reproduced and sharpened.

> Phone shop: *"The developer stopped responding properly and we had problems with the system."*
> Reason for changing provider: **"Support."** System abandoned after eight months.
> Mini-mart: *"The provider once took several days to respond to an issue."* Chose their provider
> partly because they *"had support people nearby."* What would make them stop using it:
> *"If the system becomes unreliable or support becomes poor."*
> Transport (platform): *"Changes to fares or how trips are allocated can affect income."*

**And the two "what should I have asked?" answers are both about what happens after:**

> Phone shop: *"Maybe how much support comes after you finish building something."*
> Mini-mart: *"Maybe how the company handles your information if you stop using the service."*

**CONSISTENCY** High and stable across both rounds.
**STRENGTH** High among the experienced; absent among the inexperienced. The cleanest correlation
in the study.
**WHAT IT MEANS** `[INTERPRETATION]` Abandonment is the defining negative experience of technology
purchasing in this sample, and — notably — **the customers themselves volunteered the two questions
they wish a supplier had answered.** Both concern the after-sale period. That is an unusually
direct instruction.
**WHAT IT DOES NOT MEAN** It still does not appear as a *selection* criterion at first purchase.
The phone shop chose on price and recommendation; the mini-mart on recommendation and local support
presence — which is the closest thing to support entering a purchase decision anywhere in the data.

### THEME 6 — Capacity and growth

**ROUND 1 EVIDENCE.** 2 of 2 with systems outgrew them; 3 of 3 without described it as conditional
and future.

**ROUND 2 EVIDENCE.** "If orders doubled next month, what fails first?"

| Respondent | First thing to fail | Type |
|---|---|---|
| Phone shop | Stock management and customer response | **Information** |
| Mini-mart | Staff and stock management | **Partly information** |
| Foodstuff | *"Storage would be the first problem"* | Physical |
| Transport | *"We'd need more cars"* | Physical |
| Tailoring | *"We wouldn't finish on time"* | Labour |

**Only 2 of 5 name an information failure. 3 of 5 name a physical or labour constraint that
technology cannot relieve.**

"Ever replaced something because the business got bigger?" — Phone shop: **yes, the stock system.**
Mini-mart: **yes, manual records → POS, ₦350,000.** Foodstuff: *"No major software replacement."*
Transport: *"Not software."* Tailoring: *"Bought more sewing machines."*

**Growth plans: 5 of 5** — another location, expand wholesale, more vehicles, more workers and a
bigger shop, another branch.

**Did anyone connect the growth plan to a systems need, unprompted? Zero of five.** And three of
five explicitly deferred:

> Foodstuff: *"It looks useful when the business is big, but for a smaller shop it can be
> unnecessary."* · *"I would need to be making much more money than I am now before considering it."*
> Transport: *"It makes sense when you have many vehicles."*
> Tailoring: *"It can help when you have many workers."* · *"I'd need a very big business before
> spending that."*

**Applying the Session 5 threshold:**

> ELEVATE if 3+ connect a growth plan to a systems need unprompted AND 2+ paid more or chose
> differently for anticipated growth. **DROP if the clear majority say "I'll deal with it when I
> get there."**

**0 of 5 on the first clause. 3 of 5 on the drop condition. VERDICT: DROP.**

**WHAT IT MEANS** `[EVIDENCE]` Capacity is real *retrospectively* — two businesses did replace
systems as they grew — and **absent prospectively.** Nobody buys headroom. The founders' own warning
was exactly right: *"I want my business to grow"* did **not** convert into *"I want to buy scalable
technology"* in a single case out of five. Session 3's core recommendation, "Built to do more," is
**falsified by its own pre-registered threshold.**

### THEME 7 — What they use instead *(the real competition)*

**ROUND 1 + 2 EVIDENCE.** The universal stack: **notebook + WhatsApp**, 5 of 5 across both rounds.
Excel 4 of 5. POS 1 of 5.

**Why it is tolerated:**

> *"The notebook is easy. Anybody here understands it."* (phone shop)
> *"The notebook is straightforward."* (foodstuff)
> *"It's easy to understand."* (tailoring)
> New staff can use the notebook *"immediately"* (phone shop) or in *"one day"* (tailoring).

**What it fails at — 5 of 5 said the same thing:**

> *"It doesn't automatically tell me what is selling, what is left and how much profit we're
> making."* (phone shop)
> *"It doesn't calculate everything automatically."* (foodstuff)
> *"It doesn't automatically collect everything from the different platforms."* (transport)
> *"It doesn't remind me about deadlines."* (tailoring)
> *"Some reports and deeper analysis."* (mini-mart)

**WHAT IT MEANS** `[INTERPRETATION]` **The notebook records perfectly and computes nothing.** That
is a precise and unusually clean competitive position: zero adoption cost, zero training, works
offline, universally legible to staff — and no arithmetic. Excel fails for the same reason
software does: *"too manual," "I wasn't using it consistently."*

**On WhatsApp — worth stating plainly.** What would make them stop using it? *"If customers stopped
using it"* · *"if customers moved completely to another platform."* **Nothing YBMA can do.**
`[INTERPRETATION]` WhatsApp is not displaceable in this market. Any system built for this segment
probably has to **work alongside or ingest from WhatsApp** rather than replace it — and that is a
product constraint, not a brand one.

### THEME 8 — Trust, decomposed

**ROUND 1 EVIDENCE.** Trust split five ways with no majority: understand-my-business (2),
support-that-answers (2), transparency (1), access (1), reliability (1), recommendation (1).

**ROUND 2 EVIDENCE.** The split by experience is now corroborated.

**Never-purchased (foodstuff, transport, tailoring):** what would have to be true before paying —
*"easy, affordable and actually work offline if the network is bad"* · *"show me everything without
making the drivers do too much work"* · *"it must save me time rather than give me more work."*
**All three define trust as a promise about effort and fit.**

**Purchased (phone shop, mini-mart):** what they would do differently — *"make sure I have access
to everything"* · *"ask more questions about support and data access."* **Both define it as a
promise about afterwards.**

**What would you need to see at ₦1m?**

> Phone shop: *"It would actually work, that my data is safe and that I won't need to keep paying
> somebody every time."*
> Mini-mart: *"A proper demonstration, references from businesses like mine, data security and
> clear support."*
> Transport: *"Proof that it would actually increase what I'm making."*

`[INTERPRETATION]` **At ₦1m the requirement shifts from trust to proof** — a demonstration,
references from comparable businesses, and a stated return. That is a material finding for how any
larger sale would have to be made.

**Decision-makers — 5 of 5 named someone else:** business partner and a family member · husband and
son · business partner · husband and business partner · business partner. **No purchase at this
level is made alone.**

### THEME 9 — Language

**ROUND 2 EVIDENCE — the language test, all five respondents:**

| Framing | Chose it | Named it meaningless |
|---|---|---|
| *"We sort out your records, your stock and your money."* | **3 of 5** | 0 |
| *"We help you know what's actually happening in your business."* | **2 of 5** | 0 |
| *"We build the systems your business runs on."* | 0 | 0 |
| *"We build software for businesses."* | 0 | 0 |
| ***"We do digital transformation for small and medium businesses."*** | **0** | **5 of 5** |

**Unanimous rejection of the jargon option. Unanimous preference for one of the two plain-language
outcome statements.** `[INTERPRETATION]` The split within the winners is worth noting: the two who
chose *"know what's actually happening"* are the transport operator (three drivers to oversee) and
the mini-mart (seven staff, POS in place) — the two most delegated operations. The three who chose
*"sort out your records"* are the more hands-on. `[HYPOTHESIS]` The more you delegate, the more you
want *knowing*; the more hands-on you are, the more you want *sorting out*. Weak signal at n=5, but
cheap to test.

**Caution** — stated preference for a sentence is weak evidence, and the options were written by us.
This is one signal, not a positioning.

### THEME 10 — Themes recorded as ABSENT across both rounds

# What Customers Actually Care About

## Separated as requested

**WHAT THEY BUY.** Almost nothing. Two purchases across five businesses in a combined nine to
fourteen years of trading: a ₦150,000 stock system (abandoned after eight months) and a ₦350,000
POS with recurring fees (still in use). `[EVIDENCE]`

**WHAT THEY USE.** Notebook and WhatsApp, universally. Excel, four of five. POS, one of five.
Business-critical data on a personal phone, five of five, with no tested backup.

**WHAT THEY WANT.** One number, every morning, without doing any work to get it — and in four of
five cases that number is **profit**, not revenue.

**WHAT THEY FEAR.** Three fears, in order of evidence: money and stock leaking without explanation
(5 of 5, all quantified) · a supplier taking payment and disappearing (2 of 2 buyers, unprompted) ·
buying something that becomes another job rather than removing one (*"another thing to worry
about"* · *"too much work to enter everything"*).

**WHAT THEY NEED.** `[INTERPRETATION]` Arithmetic they do not have to perform, on records they
already keep, without anyone having to do new data entry. That sentence is the synthesis of Themes
1, 2 and 7 and it is the most defensible statement in this document.

**WHAT THEY VALUE.** Things that keep working when nobody is available — a notebook any staff
member can read, a system that does not require a phone call to change. Both buyers defined
"complicated" as *needing to call someone*.

**WHAT THEY ARE WILLING TO PAY FOR.** `[EVIDENCE]` **₦100,000–₦300,000** for their single most
painful problem. Up to **₦300,000–₦700,000** for something physical whose benefit is visible. One
of five will consider ₦500,000 *"if it is really good."* **None will consider ₦1,000,000.**

## What role do they want YBMA to play?

Tested against the evidence rather than against what sounds strategic:

| Role | Verdict |
|---|---|
| **Vendor** | What they have experienced, and what failed. Both bad outcomes came from a vendor relationship that ended at delivery |
| **Developer** | `[REJECTED]` The word does not appear in any respondent's aspiration, only in their complaints. *"The developer handled that"* is said about a problem |
| **Consultant / Advisor** | `[REJECTED — decisively]` *"Have you ever paid anyone just for advice? No."* Both buyers. Zero of five have ever paid for thinking |
| **Technology partner** | `[REJECTED]` Nobody asked for a partner. It is our word and it appears nowhere in ten interviews |
| **Operator** | `[HYPOTHESIS — interesting]` *"If somebody could handle that properly and give me the correct figures, it would remove a lot of stress"* (phone shop, on stock) · *"Following up on orders. I don't want to be the person calling everyone"* (tailoring). **5 of 5 wanted to hand a records task to someone.** They may want the job done, not the tool supplied |
| **Problem solver** | Closest to their language, and low-value as a brand idea — it is what every services firm says |
| **Builder** | Honest about the activity, silent about the value |

**The strongest evidence-based answer** `[INTERPRETATION]` is uncomfortable and worth stating:
**they do not appear to want a technology supplier at all. They want the task taken away.** Five of
five, asked what they would hand to somebody tomorrow, named a records or information job — not a

# Ownership Analysis

**Against the seven options posed:**

| Option | Assessment |
|---|---|
| **A. A powerful brand idea** | **No.** One behavioural case in ten interviews. Absent from every first-purchase decision. Three of five will not pay double for it |
| **B. A differentiating promise** | **Partly — for buyers only.** 2 of 2 buyers pay double for control and both named lock-in unprompted as a thing never to do. But it is invisible to first-time buyers |
| **C. A product feature** | **Yes, and more than that** |
| **D. A trust mechanism** | **Yes — and this is the best fit.** It is what the burned buyer now uses to evaluate suppliers: *"I'd make sure I have access to everything"* |
| **E. A delivery principle** | **Yes.** It belongs in the contract, the handover and the operating standard |
| **F. An important but secondary concern** | **Yes — accurate as stated** |
| **G. Not commercially important** | **No.** Too strong. A ₦150,000 write-off and an abandonment are real costs |

## The behavioural questions, answered

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Did anyone choose one provider over another because of ownership? | **No.** Zero of five, both rounds. The phone shop chose on price and recommendation and says *"I didn't know I was supposed to ask"* |
| Did anyone pay more for ownership? | **In a hypothetical trade-off, 2 of 5 (both buyers). In reality, nobody has** |
| Did anyone leave a provider because they lacked control? | **Yes — one.** The phone shop, over dependency and support. Wrote off ₦150,000 and reverted to manual |
| Did anyone explicitly value access after delivery? | **Yes — both buyers**, unprompted, and both volunteered the after-sale question they wish they had asked |
| Did anyone know what they owned? | **No.** Phone shop: *"I'm not sure" · "I don't think so."* Mini-mart: *"not in detail."* Three others have never considered it. **Five of five have untested backups of business-critical data** |

## How strategically important is ownership to YBMA?

`[INTERPRETATION]` **Important, secondary, and conditional — and it is worth far more as a
targeting insight than as a brand idea.**

Three reasons to keep it and not lead with it:

1. **It is a second-purchase criterion.** Literacy is acquired through injury. Both buyers now say
   they would ask next time; none of them asked the first time.
2. **Reliability substitutes for it.** Two of the three refusals attach the condition *"if the
   provider is reliable."* That reframes ownership as a hedge against untrustworthy suppliers rather
   than an intrinsic good — which means a sufficiently trusted YBMA would not need to sell it.
3. **But it selects exactly the customer who can pay.** The two who value control are the two who
   have bought technology, and prior purchase is also the strongest predictor of price tolerance.

**Conclusion:** ownership is **D + E + F** — a trust mechanism, a delivery principle, and an
important secondary concern. **Session 3's promotion of it to lead proof point was too strong**, and
Session 4 already identified the hole in that reasoning. The evidence now closes it: verifiable is
not the same as recognised, and recognition arrives only after the first bad purchase.

# Visibility Analysis

**Against the six options posed:**

| Option | Assessment |
|---|---|
| **A. A common pain point** | **Yes — the most common in the study.** 5 of 5, both rounds, all quantified |
| **B. A product opportunity** | **Yes, and now demonstrated rather than assumed.** 5 of 5 have *tried* to solve it; 2 of 5 have *paid*. Session 5's decisive test is passed |
| **C. A symptom of a deeper problem** | **Partly.** In Round 1, two of five named capital and customer demand as their *first* constraint. Information sits beneath constraints YBMA cannot sell into, for some respondents |
| **D. A potential YBMA Technology proposition** | **Yes — the strongest available**, with one severe caveat: nobody valued it above ₦300,000 |
| **E. A possible master-brand idea** | **Yes — and this is the surprise.** See below |
| **F. Something else** | It is more specific than "visibility." It is **margin blindness** |

## The distinction the founders asked for

> **A common problem tells you what to build. A brand positioning tells you why to choose *us*.**

They are different tests, and visibility passes one cleanly and the other only conditionally.

- **As a problem:** outstanding. Frequent, quantified, universally attempted, twice paid for.
- **As a positioning:** weaker, for a specific reason — **every business-software vendor in the
  world claims it.** "Know your numbers" is the category's generic promise. The problem is real and
  the claim is crowded.

`[INTERPRETATION]` **What rescues it is not the promise but the mechanism.** Two respondents have
already bought reporting and found it *"limited"* and *"not really what I expected."* They did not
fail to be promised visibility; they failed to *receive* it — because receiving it required data
entry nobody sustained. **So the differentiating claim is not "you'll know your numbers." It is
"you'll know them without anyone having to do data entry."** That is Theme 2 doing the work Theme 1
cannot do alone, and it is the single most important connection in this document.

## Why it may be a master-brand idea

`[HYPOTHESIS]` Knowing what a business is actually earning — as opposed to what it is turning over
— is **precisely the job of a parent company.** A group that owns a technology business, a property
business, a farm and a lender does one thing across all four: it determines what each is really
making and allocates accordingly. This is the first idea in six sessions that is *simultaneously*
the customer's strongest stated problem and the holding company's actual function. Developed as
Territory T5.

# Simplicity Analysis

## What customers actually mean by "simple"

**Not fewer features.** The forced trade-off was unanimous for "three things," but every reason
given was about doing the needed things *well*, not about minimalism:

> *"If it does exactly what I need properly, that's better than having twenty things I don't use."*
> *"I would rather have something that does the things we need very well."*

**The real meanings, from the "too complicated" answers:**

| Meaning | Respondents | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| **Low data-entry burden** | **3 of 5** | *"Too many things before you can make one simple sale" · "five minutes after every trip" · "the same information in many places"* |
| **No dependency on the supplier** | **2 of 5 — both buyers** | *"Somebody to come every time I want to change something" · "staff need to call somebody every time"* |
| Easy to learn | Secondary | Learning times reported as days to two weeks, and nobody abandoned anything over learning |
| Familiar | Implicit | WhatsApp is the universal reference point |
| Mobile-first | **4 of 5** | *"Phone mostly"* — with computers used for records where they exist |
| Works offline | 1 of 5, strongly | *"Actually work offline if the network is bad"* (foodstuff) |
| Usable by the weakest staff member | 5 of 5 | Every respondent could name their least technical person, and what that person can manage |

**Round 1 read simplicity as a learning-curve problem. That was wrong.** It is a *running-cost*
problem: the cost of feeding the system every day, and the cost of needing the supplier.

## Is it table stakes, a differentiator, a design principle, positioning, or a segment indicator?

| Option | Verdict |
|---|---|
| **Table stakes** | **Yes, as usually claimed.** Unanimous demand plus universal vendor claim equals no differentiation |
| **A differentiator** | **Only in its specific form.** "Easy to use" differentiates nothing. *"You will not have to do data entry"* is a much harder promise that almost nobody makes |
| **A design principle** | **Yes, and non-negotiable.** Four abandonments in five businesses were caused by violating it |
| **A positioning opportunity** | **Conditionally** — via the data-entry formulation, and only if YBMA can actually deliver it |
| **A customer segment indicator** | **No — and this resolves Round 1's C1 contradiction** |

## Round 1's contradiction C1, resolved

Round 1 recorded an apparent split: R4 wanted fewer features; R5 had outgrown his system and wanted
more. **It was a false contradiction.** In Round 2 the mini-mart — the same business — still chose
"three things," and its actual complaint is that *reports are limited* and *deeper analysis* is
missing.

`[INTERPRETATION]` **It does not want more features. It wants better answers from the same inputs.**
The contradiction was created by conflating *features* with *answers*. Not a segmentation line, and
it should be removed from the state file as one.

**Applying the Session 5 threshold:** 4+ abandoned over difficulty — **PASS (4 of 5)**. Trade-off
splits along an identifiable line — **FAIL (unanimous)**. **VERDICT: ELEVATE as a product
requirement; HOLD as a differentiator.** The unanimity is the evidence of table stakes.

|---|---|

# Buying Triggers

**Only two purchases exist across both rounds. Both were reconstructed in Round 2.**

**Phone shop — ₦150,000 stock system**
> *"We were having problems knowing the exact stock and I was spending too much time checking
> things manually."*
> Specific moment: *"We had a situation where we couldn't immediately account for some accessories,
> so I decided we needed something better."*
> **Time from trigger to payment: two to three weeks.** Three providers consulted.

**Mini-mart — ₦350,000 POS and inventory**
> *"We were growing and manual records were becoming difficult."*
> Specific moment: *"We had a stock issue and realized we needed better records."*
> **Time from trigger to payment: about one month.** Three providers consulted.

## Ranked triggers

| Rank | Trigger | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | **An unexplained discrepancy in stock or money** | **2 of 2 purchases.** Both buyers named a specific unaccountable gap as the moment. **STRONG — behavioural, from actual transactions** |
| **2** | **Growth making manual records untenable** | 1 of 2, co-occurring with trigger 1, never alone |
| **3** | Accumulated time cost of manual checking | Named as pain by 5 of 5; converted to purchase only alongside trigger 1 |
| — | Losing customers · competitor pressure · customer complaints · compliance · professionalisation · investor or partner requirements | **ABSENT. Zero mentions across ten interviews** |

## What this means

`[INTERPRETATION]` **The trigger is not growth and not ambition. It is an unexplainable gap in the
numbers.** Something went missing and could not be accounted for. That is a specific, recognisable,
recurring event — and it is the moment at which a business with money will spend it.

**Two commercially important consequences:**

1. **The buying window is two weeks to one month.** Short. YBMA must be findable and credible
   *within a month* of a stock discrepancy — which argues for referral presence and local
   visibility over brand-building at this stage.
2. **Both buyers consulted exactly three providers and both chose on recommendation.** *"He was
   recommended by somebody I knew"* · *"They were recommended and had support people nearby."*
   Combined with Round 1's mini-mart (*"I didn't want to be the first person testing something"*),
   **referral is now better supported than Round 1's thin single signal** — though still from a
   small base.

## Why the non-buyers did not buy

> Foodstuff: *"I wasn't convinced I needed it badly enough."*
> Transport: *"The price and the fact that I wasn't sure it would actually save me money."*
> Tailoring: *"It looked like too much work to enter everything."*

**Three distinct barriers: insufficient pain · unproven return · anticipated data-entry burden.**
Note that the third is not a price objection at all, and it is the one nobody in the market
addresses.

# Competitive Alternatives

| Alternative | Use | Why tolerated | What it fails at | What would cause a switch |

| **Notebook** | **5 of 5** | Zero adoption cost. *"Anybody here understands it."* Usable by the least technical staff member. Works offline and in a power cut | **It does not compute.** 5 of 5 said a version of *"it doesn't calculate automatically"* | An unexplained loss large enough to demand an answer — trigger 1 |

## What YBMA would have to do differently to be worth paying for

Derived strictly from the failure modes above:

1. **Compute what the notebook cannot** — without asking anyone to abandon the notebook.
2. **Not require new data entry**, or the abandonment pattern repeats. This is the binding
   constraint and it is technical, not commercial.
3. **Work with WhatsApp**, because WhatsApp is where the data already is.
4. **Still be there in month nine.** The phone shop's system died at eight months.
5. **Be reachable without a phone call for routine tasks** — both buyers' definition of
   "complicated."
6. **Arrive within a month of a discrepancy**, via someone the owner already trusts.

`[INTERPRETATION]` Note what is *not* on that list: better design, more features, faster delivery,
higher standards, or a stronger brand. **Every item is an operational or technical commitment.**
That is the fourth consecutive session in which the evidence has pointed at delivery rather than
identity, and it is worth the founders sitting with.

## T3 — LESS WORK, NOT MORE

**CORE IDEA.** YBMA's systems take work *away* from the business rather than adding a new job to it.

**CUSTOMER PROBLEM.** **4 of 5 abandonments were caused by data-entry burden.** *"Too much work to
enter everything."* *"Nobody was entering information properly."* *"Five minutes entering
information after every trip."*

**CUSTOMER BENEFIT.** You get the figures without anybody having to do data entry.

**YBMA ROLE.** The one who removes work.

**WHY IT IS DIFFERENT.** **This is the actual failure mode of every competitor in this market, and
nobody positions against it.** Every vendor promises features and ease of use; none promises less
work. And it is the mechanism that rescues T1 from being a generic reporting claim.

**EVIDENCE SUPPORT.** 4 of 5 abandonment reasons · 5 of 5 named a records task as the thing they
would hand to somebody · tailoring's stated reason for not buying · the transport operator's staff
adoption failure · the universal survival of the notebook precisely *because* it costs nothing to
maintain.

**WEAK EVIDENCE.** **YBMA has not demonstrated it can deliver this.** Eliminating data entry means
automation, integrations and WhatsApp ingestion — technically hard, and possibly not achievable at
₦500,000. Nobody has been asked what they would pay for it specifically.

**RISK.** **The highest of any territory: it is a capability promise YBMA may not be able to keep.**
A brand built on a promise the delivery cannot meet is worse than a generic one. This must be
answered technically before it is adopted strategically.

**HOW IT COULD SCALE BEYOND TECHNOLOGY.** Moderate. *"We take work off you"* travels, but it edges
toward outsourcing and business-process services, which is a different business from what the
founders describe.

**WHAT IT COULD BECOME IN 10 YEARS.** YBMA as the group that makes businesses lighter — though this
is the least clear ten-year picture of the six.

## T4 — WHAT IT'S ACTUALLY MAKING

**CORE IDEA.** YBMA shows a business what it is actually earning, not what it is turning over.

**CUSTOMER PROBLEM.** The single most consistent experience in the study, described independently by
all five: sales looked good, the margin was much smaller than believed.

**CUSTOMER BENEFIT.** You can see the money, not just the sales.

**YBMA ROLE.** The one who tells you the truth about the money.

**WHY IT IS DIFFERENT.** Far more specific than "visibility," and it is the answer 4 of 5 gave to
*"one number you'd want every morning."* It also names something genuinely uncomfortable, which
generic claims never do.

**EVIDENCE SUPPORT.** 5 of 5, in five independent formulations. 4 of 5 named profit or net as their
one number. The two paid purchases were both attempts at this.

**WEAK EVIDENCE.** Risks reading as bookkeeping or accounting — a crowded, low-margin category that
customers may feel they already buy. Nobody in the sample pays an accountant for this, but nobody
was asked either.

**RISK.** Category confusion with accountants and bookkeepers. And it is the narrowest of the
territories as a customer proposition.

**HOW IT COULD SCALE BEYOND TECHNOLOGY.** **Excellent — the best of the six.** Determining what each
holding actually earns, and allocating accordingly, **is the literal function of a parent company.**

**WHAT IT COULD BECOME IN 10 YEARS.** YBMA as a group defined by knowing the real economics of
everything it touches. This is the strongest ten-year picture available from the evidence.

## T5 — THE SECOND SYSTEM

**CORE IDEA.** YBMA is for businesses that have already tried something and been failed by it.

**CUSTOMER PROBLEM.** *"Would you spend that again? Yes, but I would choose something different."*
Both buyers intend to buy again and both know what they got wrong.

**CUSTOMER BENEFIT.** You do not repeat the last mistake.

**YBMA ROLE.** The replacement you should have started with.

**WHY IT IS DIFFERENT.** Nobody targets the re-buyer; every competitor chases first-time buyers.

**EVIDENCE SUPPORT.** **The strongest structural finding in the study.** Prior purchase predicts

simultaneously and with no exceptions in the sample.

**WEAK EVIDENCE.** n = 2 buyers. And it is a targeting strategy rather than a brand idea.

**RISK.** The addressable market may be small. And defining YBMA by a competitor's failure is a
weak foundation for a fifteen-year brand.

**HOW IT COULD SCALE BEYOND TECHNOLOGY.** **Poorly.** It is tech-specific and does not stretch.

**WHAT IT COULD BECOME IN 10 YEARS.** Nothing, as a master brand. **Recommended as YBMA Technology's
go-to-market segmentation, not as a territory.**

## T6 — BUILT TO BE LEFT ALONE

**CUSTOMER PROBLEM.** Both buyers defined *"too complicated"* as needing to call someone. Three of

**CUSTOMER BENEFIT.** Nobody has to call anybody.

**YBMA ROLE.** The builder whose work does not need them.

**WHY IT IS DIFFERENT.** It unifies simplicity, ownership and support into a single promise — and

**EVIDENCE SUPPORT.** Good as a synthesis, thin as a standalone idea.

**WEAK EVIDENCE.** Negative-space again — Session 3's Attack 2 applies directly. Unverifiable before

**RISK.** The same trap as "built properly": a promise of adequacy dressed as a position.

**HOW IT COULD SCALE.** Good.

**WHAT IT COULD BECOME IN 10 YEARS.** Moderate. **Recommended as a delivery principle folded into
T2, not as a separate territory.**

The founders are right that an idea can serve one and damage the other. The separation:

# Single Most Important Next Test

## Recommendation: a real priced sales conversation. Not a third interview round.

**What it is.** Take the emerging proposition — *"we will make sure you always know what your
business is actually making, without your staff doing new data entry"* — and put it in front of
**five businesses that already pay monthly for business software**, with an actual price of
₦500,000–₦800,000, as a real proposal. Then watch whether anyone signs.

Recruit specifically for the profile Round 2 never included: **7–10 staff, already paying for
software, in Lagos or Abuja, in professional services, hospitality, logistics or multi-location
retail.**

## Why this has the highest information value

1. **It tests four unknowns simultaneously** — the price band, the problem, the language and the
   segment question — where every other method tests one.
2. **It replaces stated preference with revealed preference.** Every finding in six sessions about
   willingness to pay is currently someone's answer to a hypothetical. A signature is not.
3. **The interviews have stopped producing new price information.** The same five businesses have
   now answered twice and returned a consistent, clear *no* for their segment. A third round would
   confirm what we already know about people who cannot buy.
4. **It costs almost nothing.** YBMA is a services business; a proposal is a conversation and a
   document. Compare that with recruiting and running thirteen more interviews.
5. **It resolves the thing that actually blocks the brand** — whether a reachable customer exists at
   the required price — which is a commercial question that no amount of brand thinking can answer.
6. **A loss is as informative as a win.** *"Too expensive"* from a business that already pays for
   software is a much stronger signal than the same words from one that never has.

## What to do first, and it takes a day

**Before the sales conversations: answer the T3 capability question internally.** Can YBMA deliver
the correct figures without new data entry, at this price? That is a build question the founders can
answer among themselves, it gates the strongest available proposition, and it costs nothing but an
afternoon. **Do this before the pitches, because it determines what is being pitched.**

## If the founders prefer a research instrument

**Interview only the missing cell: five businesses already paying monthly for software, 7–10 staff,
outside Kaduna.** That is the one profile from the designed Round 2 that would answer the price
question. Everything else in the thirteen-respondent design is now lower priority than this single

# What We Know

Directly supported by the ten interviews. Nothing extrapolated.

1. **All five businesses described difficulty knowing what their business is actually earning.** All
   five quantified a specific loss incident, ₦25,000–₦100,000.
2. **All five named a records or information task as the thing they would hand to somebody
   tomorrow.**
3. **Four of five named profit or net income** — not revenue — as the one number they would want
   every morning.
4. **All five described the same experience:** revenue looked healthy, the actual margin was
   smaller than believed.
5. **All five have attempted to solve it. Two have paid** — ₦150,000 and ₦350,000.
6. **Both purchases were triggered by a specific unexplained discrepancy** in stock or money. Both
   moved from trigger to payment in **two to four weeks**. Both consulted **three providers**. Both
   chose on **recommendation**.
7. **Four of five abandonments were caused by data-entry or adoption burden**, not by missing
   features.
8. **Self-valuation of their single most painful problem: ₦100,000–₦300,000.** At ₦500,000, four of
   five resist or refuse. **At ₦1,000,000, none accept.** At ₦2,000,000, all five refuse.
9. **Highest technology spend in the sample: ₦350,000.** Highest-ever discretionary spend:
   ₦700,000, and it was **physical** — shop equipment.
10. **Only one of five pays anything monthly for business software** (₦20,000–₦30,000), and that is
    the only respondent who says ₦500,000 is *"possible."*
11. **Two of five pay double for full control in the trade-off. Both have bought technology. The
    three who refuse have not** — and two attach the condition *"if the provider is reliable."*
12. **Neither buyer knew what they owned.** *"I didn't know I was supposed to ask."* **All five have
    business-critical data on a personal phone with no tested backup.**
13. **Both buyers, unprompted, named the same two sins:** taking money and disappearing, and locking

14. **Both buyers volunteered the question they wish they had been asked** — and both concerned what
    happens after delivery.
15. **All five chose "three things" in the forced trade-off.** Unanimous.
16. **Zero of five connected a growth plan to a systems need unprompted**, despite all five having
    growth plans. Three of five explicitly deferred to a larger future state.
17. **Three of five said a physical or labour constraint** — storage, vehicles, hours — would fail
    first if orders doubled.
18. **All five use a notebook and WhatsApp.** All five said the notebook's failure is that it does
    not calculate.
19. **Nothing would make them leave WhatsApp except their customers leaving it.**
20. **All five named *"we do digital transformation for small and medium businesses"* as
    meaningless.** Three chose *"we sort out your records, your stock and your money."* Two chose
    *"we help you know what's actually happening in your business."*
21. **All five named someone else** — a partner, spouse or family member — as a co-decision-maker.
22. **Zero mentions of build quality, standards or rigour in ten interviews.**
23. **Zero of five has ever paid for advice alone.**
24. **Zero spontaneous mentions of websites in ten interviews.**

# What We Suspect

Strong interpretations, not proven.

1. **The problem is margin blindness, not information scarcity.** The records exist; the arithmetic
   does not.
2. **The failure mode in this market is the input, not the output.** Everyone wants a number out;
   nobody can sustain putting data in.
3. **Prior technology purchase is the market's real segmentation line.** It predicts price tolerance,
   ownership concern and dependency literacy simultaneously, with no exception in the sample.
4. **The real target may be "businesses that already pay for software and have outgrown it"** rather
   than "1–10 staff MSMEs."
5. **Reliability substitutes for ownership.** A trusted supplier does not need to sell control.
6. **Ownership is a second-purchase criterion**, acquired through injury.
7. **Physical assets clear a spending bar that software does not**, because the benefit is visible.
8. **The real competitor is a notebook, and it wins on maintenance cost, not capability.**
9. **WhatsApp is undisplaceable.** Build alongside it.
10. **They may not want a technology supplier at all** — they want the records job taken away.
11. **At ₦1m the requirement shifts from trust to proof:** a demonstration, references from
    comparable businesses, and a stated return.
12. **The more a business delegates, the more it wants *knowing*; the more hands-on, the more it
    wants *sorting out*.**

# Recommendation

## YBMA is not ready to choose a final positioning.

That is a deliberate conclusion, not a hedge — and the reason it is worth stating plainly is that
**the reason has changed, and the new reason is much better.**

For five sessions the blocker was *"we do not know what customers want."* That is no longer true.
Ten interviews have produced an unusually consistent answer: **these businesses cannot see what they
are actually earning, they have all tried to fix it, two have paid, and every attempt failed because
the solution needed feeding.** That is about as clear as qualitative evidence gets at this sample
size, and it is expressed in the customers' own words.

**The blocker now is that the segment we have researched cannot pay the price the business model
requires.** Four of five refuse ₦500,000. None accept ₦1,000,000. Their own valuation of their worst
problem is ₦100,000–₦300,000.

**That is a commercial question, not a brand question, and it has to be answered first** — because
its answer determines whether the brand is being built for these five businesses or for a materially
different customer. Choosing a positioning now would mean choosing one for a customer we have not
yet found.

## What I recommend deciding now

| Decision | Basis |
|---|---|
| **Adopt "the correct figures / what it's actually making" as the leading hypothesis** — not locked | Only hypothesis to clear its pre-registered threshold, on every clause. Their language. Scales to the parent brand without distortion |
| **Reject "Built to do more" / capacity** | Fails its threshold on every clause; triggers the drop condition. 0 of 5 |

## What must stay open

The final positioning · the essence · the promise · the customer-facing category · the price band ·

## And one thing worth saying directly

Four consecutive sessions of evidence have pointed at the same place. Every route to

not being abandoned, keeping access — is **an operational commitment, not a brand idea.** The
customers were explicit about it: *"Don't take people's money and disappear."* *"Don't lock the
business into something they can't leave."* *"Don't make something the business owner cannot
understand or access."*

That is not a brief for a logo. It is a brief for how the company works. **The identity should be
the last thing YBMA designs, and on this evidence it will be the easy part.**
