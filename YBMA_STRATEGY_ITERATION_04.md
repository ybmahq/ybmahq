## Scope and limits of this document

This iteration analyses evidence. **It does not select a positioning, an essence, a tagline or
any visual direction.**

**Sample: 5 respondents, all in Kaduna.** One has commissioned custom software. One has bought
packaged POS software. **Three have never paid for any technology to be built.**

A sample of five in one city cannot support a market claim. Throughout this document:

- **FACT** = something a respondent actually said or did.
- **INTERPRETATION** = my reading of what it may mean. Arguable.
- **HYPOTHESIS** = a proposition requiring further evidence.

Counts are given as *n of 5* and never converted into percentages or market statements. The
phrase "Nigerian SMEs want…" does not appear in this document and should not appear in any
document derived from it.

**Source:** `YBMA_SME_INTERVIEWS_ROUND_01.md` (copied into the project workspace for the record).
The transcripts were not in the session message; they were located in `~/Downloads` as
*SME Interview Responses.md*. Please confirm this is the intended and complete set.

## Three findings before the detail

**1. The strongest signal in the study is not on our hypothesis list.**
Every respondent raised difficulty *knowing what is actually happening inside their own business*
— stock, profit, orders, money. **Three of five named it as their single biggest constraint.**
After three sessions of strategy, "visibility" appears nowhere in our thinking.

**2. Both of our leading hypotheses are post-purchase themes.** `[INTERPRETATION]`
Ownership and capacity appear as **grievances after buying**, not as **criteria when buying**. Not
one respondent chose a provider on either. What decided purchases in this sample was **price and
simplicity.** This is a structural problem for a company that needs to win first jobs.

**3. Nobody in this sample discussed a website.**
Not one respondent mentioned wanting, having, or having bought a website. Web development is
YBMA's lead service. `[FACT]`

## TASK 2 — Thematic analysis

Themes are derived from the transcripts, not fitted to the prompt's list. Two themes in the

### THEME 1 — Business visibility: not knowing your own numbers

**EVIDENCE**
- R1: "Keeping track of everything. Stock is probably the biggest issue." Unaware items had run out.
- R4: "Remembering who brought what, their measurements, when they need it and whether they've paid."
- R5: "Knowing exactly how much we're making… the profit isn't what you thought."
- R2: "Managing the business properly… prices change almost every time."
- R3: "Managing the money."

**NUMBER OF RESPONDENTS** 5 of 5 mention it. **3 of 5 (R1, R4, R5) name it as their single
biggest constraint.** For R2 it is second to capital; for R3 second to customer acquisition.

**STRENGTH OF SIGNAL** **Strongest in the study.** It is the only theme present in all five, and
the only one that is the *primary* stated constraint for a majority.

**DIRECT CUSTOMER LANGUAGE**
> "You realize the profit isn't what you thought." · "Everything is in my head and my book."
> "You have plenty of some things and don't even realize another item is finished until somebody asks for it."

**POSSIBLE INTERPRETATION** `[INTERPRETATION]` The felt problem in this sample is informational,
not operational: these businesses are not failing to *do* things, they are failing to *see* them.
Notably this is distinct from capacity — R1 did not know his stock levels at his *current* volume,
not a future one.

**WHAT WE CANNOT CONCLUDE** That this is the highest-value problem to solve commercially; that
respondents would pay to solve it; that it generalises beyond five Kaduna traders; or that it is
YBMA's best entry point. Two of five named constraints (capital, customer demand) that software
cannot address at all.

### THEME 2 — Simplicity and low adoption effort

**EVIDENCE**
- R4: "If I have to sit down for one hour to learn how to use it, I probably won't use it." Plus: "too many things I don't need."
- R3: "If something is complicated, I won't use it when I'm driving." Plus: "small businesses don't have IT departments."
- R1: Rejected providers who "made it sound too complicated." Plus: "Don't build something that only the developer understands."
- R2: Would reject "if I felt the system was more complicated than what I actually needed."

**NUMBER OF RESPONDENTS** 4 of 5. Decisive in the stated selection logic of 2 (R3, R4).

**STRENGTH OF SIGNAL** **High**, and it operates as both a selection criterion and a rejection
reason — which few themes here do.

**DIRECT CUSTOMER LANGUAGE**
> "I don't want something complicated just because it's called business software."
> "Small businesses don't have IT departments."

**POSSIBLE INTERPRETATION** `[INTERPRETATION]` Adoption cost — the owner's own time and cognitive
effort — may be a larger barrier than price for some respondents, and it is entirely absent from
three sessions of YBMA strategy.

**WHAT WE CANNOT CONCLUDE** What "simple" concretely means to them; whether they would accept
fewer features in exchange; or whether simplicity survives as a priority once a business grows
(R5's case suggests it may not — see Task 10).

### THEME 4 — Provider and platform dependency

**EVIDENCE**
- R1 (provider): "We couldn't really do anything ourselves." "We shouldn't have to depend on one person just to use something we paid for." Developer held hosting. **Abandoned the system over it.**

**NUMBER OF RESPONDENTS** 3 of 5, **in three different forms** — developer dependency, platform

**STRENGTH OF SIGNAL** **Moderate-to-high where present, absent where there is no experience.**

**DIRECT CUSTOMER LANGUAGE**

**POSSIBLE INTERPRETATION** `[INTERPRETATION]` Dependency appears to be a *learned* concern. It is

**WHAT WE CANNOT CONCLUDE** That it influences purchasing (see Task 7 — it does not appear in any

### THEME 7 — Low buyer literacy *(emergent — not on the prompt's list)*

**EVIDENCE**

### Two observations that cut in opposite directions

**In ownership's favour — the evidence is behavioural, not attitudinal.** `[INTERPRETATION]` R1 did
not merely express a preference; he **stopped using a system he had paid for** because of
dependency, and reverted to a notebook. Revealed preference is the strongest evidence type
available in qualitative work, and this is the only instance of it in the study. Further: R1 raised
dependency **spontaneously at Q5**, before Q7 introduced the concept of "who held the keys" — so
it is not an artefact of the question guide.

**Against ownership — it correlates perfectly with experience, and it appears nowhere in any
purchase decision.** `[FACT]` The two respondents who have never bought technology have never
considered ownership. And no respondent — including R1 — cited ownership or control as a reason
they *chose* a provider. R1 chose on price and apparent competence, and says explicitly that he
did not know what questions to ask.

**This exposes a hole in the Session 03 reasoning.** I proposed leading acquisition with ownership
on the grounds that it is "verifiable before purchase." It is verifiable — but this evidence
suggests buyers may not know to look for it, which is a different and more serious problem than
verifiability. A differentiator the customer cannot recognise does not differentiate at the point
of sale.

### Is ownership appearing strongly enough to justify further investigation?

**Yes, clearly.** One behavioural abandonment, one strong platform-control account, and one
factual dependency case, against two respondents with no exposure to the concept. That is more than
sufficient to justify targeted research.

**It is not sufficient to conclude that ownership is a purchase driver, a customer value, or a
positioning.** On this evidence it looks like a **strong post-purchase grievance among the
experienced** and **a non-factor at the point of sale.** Whether it can be *converted* into a
purchase criterion by being made legible to buyers is a genuine open question — and, if answered
yes, potentially a large opportunity. If answered no, ownership is a retention and reputation
asset rather than a brand idea.

## TASK 4 — Capacity analysis

### DIRECT

| Respondent | Evidence |
|---|---|
| **R1** | "As the business grew, we needed something better. We have more products now and more transactions, so the old system wasn't really enough." *(Happened.)* |
| **R5** | "We eventually needed more features, especially reporting and stock management. When you're dealing with more products, you need to know which ones are moving." *(Happened.)* |
| **R3** | "If I want to build my own transport business with multiple cars, I'll need something where I can manage my own drivers and customers." *(Future.)* |

### INDIRECT / CONDITIONAL

| Respondent | Evidence |
|---|---|
| **R2** | "Right now, I can still manage most things myself, but if I have two or three shops, then it becomes more difficult." |
| **R4** | "If I get more customers, definitely… If I have more workers, I need something that everybody can use and I can still see what's happening." |
| **All 5** | Growth *ambition* is universal — a second branch (R1, R5), wholesale (R2), more cars (R3), more staff (R4). |

### ABSENT

- **No respondent expressed a desire to buy future capacity in advance.**
- **No respondent cited capacity as a reason they chose a provider or platform.**
- **No respondent described their current constraint as "my system cannot handle more."**
- **No respondent indicated they would pay more for headroom.**

### Brutally honest answer

**"Capacity/headroom" is part real phenomenon and part our construction.**

**What is real.** Outgrowing happened to **both** respondents who had systems — 2 of 2. Growth
ambition is universal — 5 of 5. Those are genuine findings and they are not nothing.

**What is ours.** The *framing* — headroom, buying room to grow into, future-proofing at purchase —
**appears nowhere in customer language.** Not one respondent asked for it, valued it, or described
wanting it. And two respondents said something close to the opposite: R2 — *"right now, I can
still manage most things myself"*; R4 — *"if I get more customers, definitely."* Both describe
buying **when the need arrives**, not before.

**Session 03's H12 was the correct worry, and this evidence leans toward confirming it.** H12
stated: *"does 'it won't break when you grow' register as valuable, or as a problem they do not
have yet?"* For the three respondents who have not bought, capacity is explicitly a future,
conditional concern.

**There is a second and more uncomfortable point.** In Session 03 I grouped the SME's problems as
"credibility, leakage, ceiling, parity" and claimed three of four were capacity problems. This
evidence says the dominant problem is what I labelled *leakage* — and leakage is not capacity, it
is **visibility.** R1 did not know his stock at his *current* volume. R5 did not know his profit at
his *current* volume. **I bundled an information problem into a capacity thesis, and the bundling
was doing more work than the evidence allows.**

**Net assessment.** Capacity survives as a real retrospective phenomenon and a legitimate
long-term group idea. It does not survive, on this evidence, as a purchase-time proposition for
this segment. It should not be developed as the core until the willingness-to-pay question (Task
12) is answered.

## TASK 5 — Trust analysis

"Trust" is not a theme in this data. It is a container holding **five distinct requirements**, and
no single one appears in a majority.

### What each respondent means by trust

| Respondent | Their definition of trust, in their framing |
|---|---|
| **R1** | Transparency (what you are doing, and what it costs) + access to my own business information + continuity if you disappear |
| **R2** | Understanding my business before selling to me — "understands the problem before talking about price" |
| **R3** | Reliability (the system will not stop working) + proper support |
| **R4** | Understanding my business — "ask me what I'm struggling with first" |
| **R5** | Recommendation and social proof (primary) + support that answers (secondary) |

### Decomposition with counts

| Component of trust | Respondents | Signal |
|---|---|---|
| Understanding the business before quoting | 2 (R2, R4) | Strong within subgroup |
| Support that actually responds | 2 (R3, R5) | Strong within subgroup |
| Continuity — not disappearing | 1 direct (R1), 1 indirect (R5) | Moderate |
| Transparent pricing and process | 1 (R1) | Weak-moderate |
| Access to own business information | 1 (R1) | Weak-moderate |
| System reliability | 1 (R3) | Weak-moderate |
| Recommendation / social proof | 1 (R5) | Weak-moderate |
| Technical competence | 1 (R1, "seemed like he knew what he was doing") | Weak |

### The structural finding

`[INTERPRETATION]` **Trust requirements in this sample split by whether the respondent has bought
technology before.**

- **Never purchased (R2, R4):** trust means *understand me before you sell to me.* Their fear is
  being sold the wrong thing.
- **Has purchased or uses platforms (R1, R3, R5):** trust means *do not leave me stranded.* Their
  fear is abandonment.

If this holds at larger n, it has a direct operational consequence: **the behaviours that win a
first-time buyer are not the behaviours that retain an experienced one.** Diagnostic listening
acquires; visible support retains.

### Strongest concrete trust-creating behaviours the evidence points to

Ranked by evidence, not by appeal:

1. **Ask about the business problem before quoting a price.** (R2, R4 — both explicit, both primary)
2. **Answer when something goes wrong.** (R3, R5 — both explicit)
3. **State clearly what you are doing and what it costs.** (R1)
4. **Give the client access to their own business information.** (R1)
5. **Be referenceable — have other local businesses using you successfully.** (R5)
6. **Do not disappear after payment.** (R1, R5)

Note that items 1–6 are all **behaviours**, not messages. None of them is a brand claim.

## TASK 10 — Contradictions

Not averaged away.

### C1 — Simplicity vs capability *(the sharpest one)*

**R4:** would reject software with "too many things I don't need."
**R5:** outgrew his system and "eventually needed more features, especially reporting and stock
management."

Same market, opposite directions. Build simple and the R5s outgrow it; build capable and the R4s
never adopt it. **This is a genuine product-strategy problem, not a messaging problem**, and it
cannot be resolved by positioning. `[HYPOTHESIS]` A staged or modular approach — usable on day
one, extensible later — is the obvious candidate resolution, and it is worth noting that such an
approach would draw on *both* the simplicity finding and the capacity finding. That is a research
question, not a recommendation, and it is deliberately not developed further here.

### C2 — Control vs safety in numbers

**R3:** wants to escape platform control and own his customer relationships.
**R5:** explicitly wants to be on what others already use — "I didn't want to be the first person
testing something."

A brand built on independence appeals to R1 and R3 and mildly repels R5. **These may be different
customers, not one customer with mixed feelings.**

### C3 — Ownership matters vs never considered

**R1:** abandoned a paid system over dependency.
**R2, R4:** have never thought about it.

Ownership is not a universal value in this sample. It is an **experienced** value. Any strategy
built on it must answer: do we sell to the minority who have been burned, or do we teach the
majority to care?

### C4 — Price-led vs reliability-led

**R2:** price is the binding constraint.
**R3:** never mentioned price once; framed everything around reliability, ease and benefit.

One respondent in five did not treat price as the issue. **Do not conclude price is universal**,
even in a sample this price-conscious.

### C5 — Diagnostic listening vs social proof

**R2, R4:** trust is earned by understanding my business first. Implies a consultative,
bespoke model.
**R5:** trust is earned by other businesses already using it. Implies a standardised, product
model.

**These are opposite trust mechanisms and they imply different business models.** A consultative
approach cannot easily generate the "everyone around here uses them" proof R5 requires; a
productised approach cannot easily deliver the bespoke listening R2 and R4 require.

### C6 — Growth ambition vs current adequacy

**All five** want to grow. **Three of five** say they can manage adequately right now.

**Ambition does not equal purchase urgency.** This directly weakens any strategy that assumes
growth intent converts into demand for growth-enabling technology.

### What the contradictions mean for YBMA

`[INTERPRETATION]` Five respondents produced six genuine contradictions. That is not noise from a
small sample — it suggests this segment is **not one market.** The most likely reading is that
"Nigerian SMEs" is too coarse a target to design a single proposition around, and that the real
segmentation variable may be **prior technology experience** (which predicts ownership concern,
support concern and trust type) rather than sector or size.

That is a hypothesis worth testing directly, and it would change the audience decision confirmed
in Session 02.

## TASK 12 — What the interviews do not tell us

Ranked by strategic value of the answer.

### Tier 1 — answers would change the strategy

## TASK 13 — Research quality check

### What is represented

| | |
|---|---|
| **Sectors** | Phone/gadget retail + repair · foodstuff retail/wholesale · transport/ride-hailing · tailoring · mini-mart general retail |
| **Geography** | **Kaduna only — 5 of 5** |
| **Size** | All appear owner-operated or family-run. **These read as micro-enterprises rather than the mid-sized SMEs that commission software** `[INTERPRETATION]` |
| **Model** | All B2C or light B2B. Retail, trade and personal services |
| **Purchase history** | **1 custom build (R1) · 1 packaged software (R5) · 3 never purchased (R2, R3, R4)** |
| **Technology intensity** | High (R3), moderate (R1, R5), low (R2, R4) |

### What is missing — and this list matters more than what is present

- **Any business outside Kaduna.** No Lagos, Abuja or Port Harcourt. Price points, buyer literacy
  and competitive intensity plausibly differ.
- **Any professional-services SME** — clinic, school, law firm, accountancy, logistics, agency.
  These are the SMEs that commission websites and software and hold budgets.
- **Any business that has spent meaningful money on a custom build.** R1's was "a small system."
- **Any B2B, manufacturing or distribution business.**
- **Any business with a real headcount** using a shared system — R4 described wanting exactly this
  and does not have it.
- **Any business that has successfully switched providers.** Nobody has.
- **Anyone who has bought a website.** Zero. This is a striking gap for YBMA's lead service.
- **Any school, NGO or government-adjacent buyer.**
- **Any satisfied technology customer.** The only two experiences reported are negative. We have no
  picture of what good looks like to these buyers.

### Selection biases to worry about

1. **Single-city sample.** The largest limitation. Nothing here can be described as a Nigerian
   finding.
2. **Segment mismatch.** These businesses are smaller than the customers who would fund YBMA. The
   audience decision confirmed in Session 02 says "Nigerian SMEs"; this sample may be testing a
   different segment than the one being targeted.
3. **Non-buyer weighted.** 3 of 5 have never purchased, so most "buying" evidence is hypothetical.
   Hypothetical purchase answers are weak evidence and systematically over-emphasise price.
4. **Sector concentration** in retail and trade. No services.
5. **Recruitment unknown.** `[HYPOTHESIS]` If respondents were sourced through the founders'
   network, expect profile similarity and softened criticism. **Please tell me how they were
   recruited** — it changes how much weight this round carries.
6. **Interviewer effect.** The founders are researching a business they intend to build. Negative
   feedback about "technology companies" is easy to give; negative feedback that implicates the
   interviewer is not.
7. **Question-guide issues.** The guide was not branched — R2 and R4 were asked four questions
   about experiences they had not had, producing five "I haven't had that experience" answers and
   losing interview time that could have gone to their actual situation. Worth fixing before the
   next round.
8. **One prompting risk, and one reassurance.** Q7 explicitly introduces "who held the keys,"
   which primes the ownership concept. **However:** R1 raised dependency spontaneously at Q5,
   before that prompt. His evidence is therefore not an artefact of the guide. R5's and R3's
   ownership answers *were* prompted and should carry less weight.

### Recommended next 5–10 interviews — specific profiles

| # | Profile | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Owner who paid a developer and later lost access to their domain, hosting or code.** Find via the "site went down and we couldn't reach him" pattern | Tests ownership at its sharpest. The only profile that can tell us what lock-in actually costs |
| 2 | **Owner who successfully switched developers or platforms** | Tells us what transferred, what did not, and what the switch cost. Nobody in round 1 has done this |
| 3 | **Professional-services SME (clinic, school, law firm, logistics) that has paid ₦500k+ for software or a website** | The budget segment. Round 1 contains nobody who has spent real money |
| 4 | **Owner who considered custom software and chose a template, platform or nothing, on price** | Tests the price barrier directly and tells us what would have changed their mind |
| 5 | **Owner who outgrew a platform (Shopify, Selar, a Paystack storefront) and had to move** | Tests capacity where it actually bit, with a buyer who has felt it |
| 6 | **A Lagos business in the same sector as one Kaduna respondent** | Isolates the city variable. The cheapest way to learn whether round 1 generalises |
| 7 | **An SME with 5–20 staff where several people use one system** | R4's stated future need. Tests the multi-user visibility case |
| 8 | **A business paying a monthly retainer or support fee to a tech provider** | **The only realistic route to willingness-to-pay evidence** for continuity and support |
| 9 | **An owner who specifically bought a website** | YBMA's lead service, entirely absent from round 1 |
| 10 | **A freelance developer or small agency serving this market** | Supply-side view: why lock-in happens, what buyers actually ask for, and what price points clear |

### Questions to add — and one method change

**On willingness to pay** (the biggest gap). Never ask "would you pay more for X." Ask:
- "What did you pay, and what did you expect for that?"
- "If one quote was ₦X and another was ₦2X with [specific difference], which would you take, and why?"
- "What is the most you have ever spent on anything for the business that was not stock or rent?"

**On ownership — test it behaviourally, not attitudinally.** Everyone says yes to "would you like
to own your data." Instead ask:
- "Whose name is your domain registered in?" *(Most will not know. That is the finding.)*
- "If your developer stopped answering tomorrow, what would you do?"
- "Who can log in and change your prices right now?"
- **"Has anything you paid for ever stopped being usable?"**

**On simplicity** — make it concrete:
- "Show me how you record a sale now." *(Observe, do not ask.)*
- "What is the last app you stopped using, and why?"

**Method change:** branch the guide. Two versions — one for buyers, one for non-buyers — so no
respondent spends four questions saying "I haven't had that experience."

It remains operationally useful. Session 03's demotion of it looks correct. |
| **"Built to do more" / Capacity** | **MORE RESEARCH — and reframe** | The phenomenon is real (2 of 2 with systems outgrew them). The *purchase-time* framing is not supported and is contradicted by R2 and R4. Do not develop as the core until Tier-1 question 4 is answered. |
| **Ownership / Control** | **MORE RESEARCH — highest priority** | Highest distinctiveness on the board, one piece of genuine behavioural evidence (R1's abandonment), and spontaneous rather than prompted. But absent from every purchase decision and perfectly correlated with prior experience. The open question is whether it can be *made* legible to buyers. |
| **Transparency** | **MORE RESEARCH** | One strong source (R1), cheap to test, plausibly bundles with ownership. |
| **Trust** | **DEVELOP — but only decomposed** | As a word it is unusable. As five behaviours it is the most actionable material in this round. Develop the two with strongest support: **diagnose before quoting**, and **answer when something breaks.** |
| **Simplicity** | **DEVELOP** | 4 of 5, decisive for 2, and one of the top two purchase barriers. **The most under-weighted theme in all prior work.** It should be promoted to a first-class strategic consideration. |
| **NEW — Business visibility** | **MORE RESEARCH → strong DEVELOPMENT candidate** | The strongest signal in the study: 5 of 5 mention, 3 of 5 name it their biggest constraint. Absent from three sessions of strategy. Needs willingness-to-pay evidence before development. |
| **NEW — Support / continuity** | **DEVELOP** as an operational commitment | 3 of 3 among respondents with any technology experience. Concrete, ownable, and it addresses the one experience both buyers actually had. |
| **NEW — Low buyer literacy** | **DEVELOP** as a market condition | Not a positioning. But it plausibly explains why price dominates, and why ownership cannot yet function as a purchase criterion. It shapes how anything is sold. |

### The honest structural problem

`[INTERPRETATION]` **Both hypotheses we have been developing for two sessions are post-purchase
themes.** Ownership and capacity are things customers come to care about *after* buying. What
decided purchases in this sample was price and simplicity.

A brand needs to do two different jobs — win the first job, and earn the second. On this evidence
our strategy has been optimising for the second while assuming it would also deliver the first.
**That assumption is not supported and should be treated as broken until tested.**

### And a caution about this sample specifically

The audience confirmed in Session 02 was "Nigerian SMEs." This round interviewed five
owner-operated micro-enterprises in one city, three of whom have never bought technology.
`[INTERPRETATION]` **It is possible this round has tested a different segment than the one YBMA
intends to serve** — in which case the findings are valid and the target needs re-specifying. That
question should be settled before round 2 is designed, because it determines who to recruit.

## TASK 15 — What would change our mind

Falsification conditions for the three leading hypotheses, defined **before** further research.

### If OWNERSHIP is the leading hypothesis — we should abandon it if:

1. **Five more buyers, asked "whose name is your domain registered in?", say they do not know —
   and, asked "what would you do if your developer stopped answering?", are untroubled.** That
   would establish ownership as a concern of the burned minority rather than a latent market need.
2. **We find buyers who were explicitly offered full ownership and chose the cheaper locked-in
   option anyway.** This is the decisive test. If ownership loses to price when both are on the
   table and understood, it is dead as a positioning.
3. **Experienced buyers say that, knowing what they know, they would still choose on price.**
4. **Switchers report that switching was easy**, which would mean lock-in is a smaller cost than
   R1's case suggests.

*If 1–3 hold, ownership becomes a retention and reputation asset — a contractual commitment worth
making and not a brand idea.*

### If CAPACITY is the leading hypothesis — we should abandon it if:

1. **Five more respondents say some version of "I'll deal with that when I get there."** R2 and R4
   have already said it. Three more would settle it.
2. **Businesses that outgrew systems simply bought again without resentment** — which would make
   outgrowing a normal cost of doing business rather than a grievance to build on.
3. **Buyers offered a cheaper limited build and a costlier extensible one choose the cheaper one.**
4. **The binding constraint turns out to be capital or demand rather than systems** — which R2 and
   R3 have already indicated for themselves.

*Any two of these should end capacity as a purchase-time proposition, while leaving it available as
a long-term group idea.*

### If VISIBILITY is the leading hypothesis — we should abandon it if:

1. **Further respondents say the notebook works fine** and name capital, demand, competition or
   power as the real constraint. Note that **2 of 5 already led with constraints software cannot
   solve.** Three more would break it.
2. **Owners say they know their numbers well enough** and do not experience the R5 "profit isn't
   what you thought" moment.
3. **Owners will not pay for information** — treating record-keeping as their own job rather than
   something to buy.
4. **Existing cheap tools already solve it** — if respondents are content with WhatsApp, a
   notebook and a POS terminal, there may be no gap to sell into.

*This one is the most likely of the three to survive contact with more evidence, and also the one
most at risk of being a symptom rather than the constraint.*

### A general falsification condition for the whole project

**If round 2 produces another set of contradictions as strong as C1–C6, the honest conclusion is
that "Nigerian SMEs" is not one market** — and the correct next step is segmentation, not
positioning. On current evidence the most promising segmentation variable is **prior technology
experience**, not sector, size or city.

# FINAL OUTPUT

## What We Know

Directly supported by the five interviews. Nothing here is extrapolated.

1. **All five respondents described difficulty knowing what is happening inside their own
   business** — stock (R1), orders and measurements (R4), profit (R5), prices and management (R2),
   money (R3). **Three of five named it as their single biggest constraint.**
2. **Two of five have paid for technology.** R1 commissioned a custom build; R5 bought packaged POS
   software. **Three have never purchased anything.**
3. **R1 abandoned a system he had paid for because he was dependent on the developer**, and
   reverted to manual methods. He raised this spontaneously, before any question about ownership.
4. **Both respondents who had systems outgrew them.** R1 needed more as products and transactions
   grew; R5 needed reporting and stock management.
5. **Price was cited as a selection or rejection factor by four of five.** R3 never mentioned it.
6. **Simplicity and adoption effort were cited by four of five**, and were decisive in the stated
   logic of two (R3, R4). R4 gave a specific ceiling: one hour to learn is too much.
7. **Two respondents have never considered who owns or controls what they would buy.** R2: "I've
   never really thought about it." R4: "I don't really know."
8. **All three respondents with any technology experience raised abandonment or support.** Neither
   of the two without experience did.
9. **The two respondents who have never purchased both named "understand my business before
   selling to me" as their primary trust requirement.**
10. **One respondent named recommendation and local proof as his primary trust factor** and said he
    did not want to be first to test something.
11. **No respondent used quality or build-standard language.** Nobody described anything as badly
    built.
12. **No respondent cited ownership, control or capacity as a reason they chose a provider.**
13. **No respondent mentioned a website** — not having one, wanting one, or buying one.
14. **No respondent named a budget, a price paid, or a price ceiling.**
15. **Two of five named primary constraints that software cannot address** — capital (R2) and
    customer demand (R3).

## What We Suspect

Strong interpretations, not proven.

1. **The felt problem in this segment is informational rather than operational** — not an inability
   to do things, but an inability to see them. Distinct from capacity.
2. **Ownership is a learned concern, not an intuitive one.** It appears only among those with
   technology experience and never in a purchase decision.
3. **Both of our leading hypotheses are post-purchase themes.** They describe what customers come
   to value, not what makes them buy.
4. **Low buyer literacy may be the mechanism that makes price dominate.** Where quality cannot be
   assessed, price is the only comparable variable.
5. **Trust requirements split by prior experience** — "understand me" before purchase, "don't
   abandon me" after.
6. **Prior technology experience may be a better segmentation variable than sector, size or city**,
   because it predicts ownership concern, support concern and trust type.
7. **Adoption effort may be a barrier comparable to price** for some buyers, and it is absent from
   all prior YBMA strategy.
8. **The real competitor is a notebook**, not another software firm. R4: "everything is in my head
   and my book."
9. **"Nigerian SMEs" may be too coarse a target** to design one proposition around — six
   substantive contradictions from five people.
10. **This round may have sampled a smaller segment than YBMA intends to serve.**

## What We Don't Know

1. **Whether anyone would pay more for anything.** Zero pricing evidence. This undermines the
   Session 03 pricing decision, which should be re-labelled an assumption.
2. **Whether ownership can be made to matter before purchase.** The highest-value unknown.
3. **Whether lock-in changes a purchase decision** when the buyer understands it.
4. **Whether capacity matters at the point of purchase.** All current evidence says no.
5. **Whether SMEs distinguish owning from having access.**
6. **What "simple" concretely means** beyond R4's one-hour ceiling.
7. **What happens when an SME changes providers.** Nobody has.
8. **Whether any of this holds outside Kaduna.**
9. **What SMEs with real software budgets want.** Nobody in this sample has one.
10. **Why websites never came up**, given they are YBMA's lead service.
11. **What good looks like** — both reported experiences were negative.
12. **How these five were recruited**, which determines how much weight the round carries.

## Recommended Next Research

**Ten profiles**, in priority order — full detail in Task 13:

1. Owner who lost access to a domain, hosting or code after paying a developer
2. Owner who successfully switched providers
3. Professional-services SME that has paid ₦500k+ for software or a website
4. Owner who rejected custom software on price and bought a template or nothing
5. Owner who outgrew a platform and had to migrate
6. A Lagos business matched to a Kaduna respondent's sector
7. An SME with 5–20 staff sharing one system
8. A business paying a monthly support retainer *(the only route to willingness-to-pay evidence)*
9. Someone who specifically bought a website
10. A freelance developer or small agency serving this market

**Three method changes:**

- **Test ownership behaviourally, never attitudinally.** "Whose name is your domain in?" not "do
  you value ownership?" Attitudinal questions on ownership produce false positives.
- **Ask the money questions directly.** "What did you pay?" · "₦X versus ₦2X with this difference —
  which, and why?" · "Most you've ever spent on anything for the business other than stock or rent?"
- **Branch the guide** into buyer and non-buyer versions, so nobody spends four questions saying
  "I haven't had that experience."

**And one question for the founders:** how were these five recruited? It determines how much
weight this round can carry.

## Strategic Implication

**No positioning is selected, and none should be on this evidence.**

Where this leaves the project:

**1. The two hypotheses we have developed for two sessions are not purchase drivers in this
sample.** Ownership and capacity are what customers come to value after buying. Price and
simplicity are what decided purchases. A brand has to win the first job and earn the second; our
strategy has been optimising for the second while assuming it delivered the first.

**2. The strongest signal in the study was outside our frame entirely.** Visibility — not knowing
your own stock, orders or profit — was mentioned by all five and named as the primary constraint by
three. Three sessions of reasoning never reached it. That is worth sitting with: it is evidence
that the gap between our analysis and the customer was real, and larger than the gap between our
competing hypotheses.

**3. Ownership is now more interesting, not less — but for a different reason than we thought.** It
carries the study's only piece of behavioural evidence, spontaneously raised, and it retains the
highest distinctiveness potential on the board. But it is a grievance of the experienced rather
than a criterion of the buying. The real question is no longer "do customers value ownership" but
**"can ownership be made legible to a buyer who does not know to ask?"** That is a research
question with a large commercial prize attached, and it is the single thing I would test next.

**4. Simplicity deserves promotion to a first-class consideration.** Four of five, decisive for
two, and a top-two purchase barrier. It has been absent from every prior session.

**5. The audience may need re-specifying before anything else.** This round sampled five
owner-operated micro-enterprises in one city, three of whom have never bought technology. That may
not be the segment YBMA intends to serve. **Settle this before designing round 2**, because it
determines who to recruit — and it bears on a decision already marked CONFIRMED in Session 02.

**6. What should not change.** The architecture work (branded house, division naming, endorsed
acquisitions) is unaffected by this evidence — it was never customer-derived. The internal
operating standard is unaffected and remains useful. Both can proceed.

**7. What should not proceed.** No essence, no positioning, no promise and no visual work until
round 2. The pattern of three recommendations in three sessions was flagged in Session 03 as
something to interrogate; this round is the reason it was worth flagging. **The project is still
blocked on evidence, and it is now blocked on a more specific and more answerable question than
before** — which is progress, even though it does not feel like it.
