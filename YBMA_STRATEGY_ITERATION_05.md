## Before the design: one structural tension that Round 2 must resolve

**The target definition may contain a contradiction, and it is more important than any brand
hypothesis.**

Round 1's respondents were a phone shop, a foodstuff trader, an owner-driver, a tailor and a
mini-mart. `[INFERENCE]` For most of them, ₦500,000 is plausibly a significant fraction of a
month's turnover. R2 said *"sometimes the software is not the problem; the price is the problem."*
R4 rejects things for being *"too expensive."*

So: **does the set of Nigerian businesses with 1–10 staff meaningfully intersect the set of
businesses that will spend ₦500k–₦2m on technology?** If the intersection is thin, the target is
not "1–10 staff MSMEs" — it is *"the top slice of MSMEs by money-at-risk,"* which is a completely
different recruitment brief, a different sales motion and a different brand.

**This is not a reason to change the target. It is Round 2's first research question.** It is
handled as CRITICAL priority 1 (Task 15) and it drives one specific design decision:

> ### Recruitment principle: screen on money-at-risk, not headcount.
>
> `[INFERENCE]` Headcount is a poor proxy for technology budget in Nigeria. A three-person
> distributor moving ₦30m of stock a month is a far better prospect than a nine-person restaurant.
> Screen on transaction volume, stock value, locations, vehicles and prior discretionary spend —
> then record headcount as a descriptor, not a filter.

## 1 — Round 2 research objective

> **What do 1–10 person Nigerian businesses need from a technology partner badly enough to spend
> ₦500,000–₦2,000,000 — and which of them can actually do it?**

Three sub-objectives, in order of importance:

1. **Establish whether the price band and the customer definition are compatible**, and if so, what
   distinguishes the businesses at which they are.
2. **Identify the recurring job** worth that money — expressed as a business problem, not a
   technology category.
3. **Test the five standing hypotheses behaviourally**: visibility, ownership, simplicity,
   capacity, and the standards/quality idea.

**Explicitly not an objective:** confirming any prior recommendation. Round 1 produced its two
strongest findings (visibility, simplicity) precisely because the analysis was not run to validate
the existing strategy. That discipline is carried forward — see the pre-registered thresholds in
section 17, which are written *before* the interviews so they cannot be adjusted afterwards.

### Recruitment screen — ask before booking

Behavioural, non-invasive, and designed to find money-at-risk rather than headcount:

1. How many people work in the business, including you?
2. Roughly how many customers or orders in a normal week?
3. Do you have more than one location, or vehicles?
4. Do any of your staff need to use the same records that you do?
5. **"Have you ever paid more than ₦200,000 for anything for the business that wasn't stock, rent,
   vehicles or salaries? What was it?"**
6. Have you ever paid anyone to build a website, an app, or a system for the business?

**Question 5 is the critical screen.** It establishes capacity for discretionary spend
behaviourally, without asking about revenue — which is intrusive, frequently refused, and often
inaccurate. A business that has never spent ₦200k discretionarily is very unlikely to spend ₦1m,
and that fact is itself a finding worth recording rather than screening away silently.

**Keep a recruitment log** of everyone screened and not interviewed, with the reason. Round 1's
biases were invisible because there was no log.

## 3 — Geographic distribution

Round 1 was 5 of 5 Kaduna. The objective is **not national representation** — it is determining
whether the Kaduna findings are location-specific.

### Recommended: three cities, weighted

| City | Respondents | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Kaduna** | **4** | Continuity with Round 1. Allows direct same-sector comparison and tests whether Round 1's findings reproduce |
| **Lagos** | **6** | The maximally different market — higher price tolerance, more suppliers, more tech-literate buyers, far more competition. **If a finding holds in both Kaduna and Lagos, it is probably national. If it diverges, that divergence is itself a segmentation finding** |
| **Abuja** | **3** | Different again — services-heavy, more formal, government-adjacent, higher administrative compliance. Also the market where YBMA's longer-term institutional ambitions would eventually live |

### Why three and not five

Three cities at 3–6 each gives enough per city to see a pattern. Five cities at 2–3 each gives
anecdotes everywhere and patterns nowhere. **At this sample size, depth beats spread.**

### The key methodological device: matched pairs

Interview **the same sector in two cities**, so any difference is attributable to location rather
than sector:

| Pair | Sector | Cities |
|---|---|---|
| **P1** | Phones/gadgets or comparable small retail | Kaduna (R1's own sector) ↔ Lagos (#3) |
| **P2** | Boutique / fashion | Kaduna (R4's sector) ↔ Abuja (#8) |
| **P3** | Foodstuff / distribution | Kaduna (#6) ↔ Lagos (#2) |

Three matched pairs cost nothing extra and convert "we interviewed people in different cities"
into "we can attribute differences to city." Without this, a Lagos–Kaduna difference is
uninterpretable — it could be the city or the sector.

### If access is constrained

**Drop Abuja, not Lagos.** Kaduna + Lagos gives the two most contrasting markets and preserves the
matched-pair logic. Two contrasting markets studied properly beats three studied shallowly.

## 4 — Interview guide

**Approximately 60 minutes.** Can be cut to 45 by dropping Sections 6 and 7 — see section 18 for
what is non-negotiable.

### Governing rules — read before every interview

1. **Ask about the last time, not the general case.** *"Tell me about the last time…"* rather than
   *"do you usually…"* Recalled specifics are reliable; self-reported generalities are not.
2. **Ask for artefacts. "Can you show me?"** The notebook, the POS screen, the WhatsApp order
   thread, the spreadsheet. **Observation outranks testimony** and produces the strongest evidence
   class in the framework.
3. **Never name a hypothesis.** The words *ownership, simplicity, capacity, visibility, standards,
   quality, growth* must not be spoken by the interviewer. If the respondent says one first, you
   may follow it — and **record that they said it first.**
4. **Record where each theme is first mentioned.** First-mention position is data: a theme raised
   unprompted in Section 1 is far stronger evidence than the same theme agreed to in Section 5.
5. **Use silence.** After an answer, wait. The second answer is usually the real one.
6. **Convert every adjective into a number.** How many, how long, how much, how often.
7. **Ask about money last.** Introducing price early makes everything afterwards about price.
8. **You are not selling.** Do not pitch, quote, or take deposits. If asked for a quote, say this
   is research and offer a separate conversation afterwards.
9. **Write verbatim.** Their language is a deliverable, not a means to one. Record if permitted.

### SECTION 0 — Context *(5 min)*

- Tell me about the business — what do you sell or do?
- How many people work here including you, and what does each of them do?
- How long have you been running it?
- How many customers or orders in a normal week?
- More than one location? Vehicles?

---

### SECTION 1 — The job *(10 min)* — **ASK FIRST, PROMPT NOTHING**

This section exists to capture what the respondent raises **unprompted**. It is the most important
section in the guide and the one whose data carries the most weight.

- **"Walk me through yesterday. What did you actually do, hour by hour?"**
  *(The best single question in the guide. It surfaces the real work without asking anyone to
  theorise about it.)*
- What part of running this business takes more time than it should?
- What goes wrong most often?
- **When was the last time something went wrong that cost you money? What happened?**
- Roughly how much did that cost?
- What did you do about it? Did anything change afterwards?
- If you could hand one part of this business to somebody else tomorrow, what would it be, and why
  that part?

> **After this section, before moving on, write down every theme the respondent raised without
> being asked.** That list is the highest-value data in the interview.

---

### SECTION 2 — Visibility *(8 min)*

Behavioural throughout. Do not ask whether information is a problem — find out.

- Right now, without checking — how much stock do you have of your best-selling item?
  *(Then: can you check? How long did that take?)*
- How much money did the business make last week? How do you know that?
- **"Walk me through how you work out whether you made a profit last month — actually walk me
  through it."**
- Who owes you money right now, and how much? How do you know?
- **Show me where that information lives.** *(Ask to see it. Photograph it with permission.)*
- When was the last time one of those numbers surprised you? What happened?
- Has money or stock ever gone missing without you noticing? How did you find out?
- What's the one number you'd want to see every morning without doing any work to get it?
- **Have you ever tried to fix this? What did you try? What happened?**
- **Did you pay for anything to fix it? How much?**

The final two questions are the test that separates a painful problem from a symptom. See
section 8.

---

### SECTION 3 — Current alternatives *(5 min)*

- What do you use to keep records right now? **Show me.**
- Who set that up — you, staff, family, someone you paid?
- What does it do well?
- What does it not do?
- Has anyone in the business suggested doing it differently?
- Do you use WhatsApp for business? For what exactly — orders, payments, records, customer chat?
- **Are you paying for any app or software right now, even something small? How much a month?**
- Has a family member or a member of staff ever built or set something up for you?

That paid-software question establishes the existing technology spend baseline. **Round 1 never
asked it, so we have no idea what these businesses already pay for.**

---

### SECTION 4 — BRANCH *(12 min)*

**Use 4A or 4B. Do not use both.** Round 1's guide was unbranched and two respondents spent four
questions saying *"I haven't had that experience."*

#### 4A — Has purchased technology

- Tell me about the last technology you paid for. What was it?
- **Take me back to before you bought it. What was happening in the business that made you start
  looking?**
- Was there a specific moment or event? *(Buying trigger — section 13.)*
- Who did you talk to? How many options did you consider?
- **How much did you pay?** *(Ask directly. Do not soften it.)*
- How did you pay — all at once, in parts, monthly?
- Why that provider and not the others?
- What did the others quote?
- What did you expect to get for the money?
- What did you actually get?
- **Is it still in use today?** *(If not: when did it stop, and why?)*
- Who uses it — you, or staff too? How many people?
- What did it change about the business? Anything you can measure?
- Was it worth the money? Why?
- Would you spend that again — on the same thing, or something different?
- What would you do differently?

#### 4B — Has never purchased technology

- Has anyone ever tried to sell you software or a website? What happened?
- Have you ever come close to buying something? What stopped you?
- **"What's the most you've ever spent on anything for the business that wasn't stock, rent,
  vehicles or salaries? What was it?"**
- Was that worth it? How did you decide?
- Do you know a business like yours that uses software? What do you make of their setup?
- If something existed that fixed *[the problem they named in Section 1, in their words]* — what
  would have to be true before you'd pay for it?
- Who would you talk to before spending that kind of money?

### SECTION 5 — Ownership *(7 min)*

**Placed after Section 1–4 deliberately**, so ownership has had four chances to arise unprompted.
Round 1's guide introduced the concept at Q7 and that weakened two respondents' evidence.

#### For those with a website, system or paid software

- Do you have a website or any online presence? **Whose name is the domain registered in?**
  *(If unsure: do you know where it was bought? Do you have the login?)*
- Who pays for the hosting — whose card or account is it on?
- Do you have the login details for everything? **Can you show me you can log in?**
- Who else has access?
- Your customer list and records — where do they physically live? If you wanted to move them
  somewhere else tomorrow, could you?
- **"If the person who built it stopped answering your calls tomorrow, what would happen? Walk me
  through the first week."**
- Has anything like that ever happened? Tell me about it.
- **Have you ever changed developers or providers?** If yes:
  - What made you change?
  - What did you have to rebuild or pay for a second time?
  - **How much did that cost you?**
  - How long were you without it?
  - Did you get your data out? How?
- Did anyone explain what you would own before you paid? **Did you ask?**

#### For those with no purchase history — the platform adaptation

Every non-buyer already has platform dependency through WhatsApp and Instagram. Ownership can
therefore be tested without a purchase history:

- Your business records — if your phone was stolen tomorrow, what would you lose?
- Your WhatsApp or Instagram business account — if it was locked tomorrow, what happens to the
  business?
- Has a platform ever changed something that hurt your business? What did you do?
- Your customer contacts — where are they? Could you get them off that phone?

`[INFERENCE]` R3 gave us exactly this evidence via ride-hailing platforms in Round 1. Extending the
logic to WhatsApp and Instagram means **ownership becomes testable across the whole sample**, not
just the buyers — which was the main limitation of Round 1's ownership evidence.

### SECTION 6 — Simplicity *(6 min)*

- What's the last app or system you learned to use — business or personal?
- How long before you were comfortable with it? Hours? Days?
- What did you find hardest?
- Did someone teach you, or did you work it out?
- **What's the last app you stopped using? Why?**
- Of what you use now, which parts do you never touch?
- **Who in the business is worst with technology? What can they manage?**
  *(This sets the real usability floor — not the owner's ability, the weakest user's.)*
- If a new staff member started on Monday, how long before they could use your current records?
- Do you do business things on your phone or on a computer? Which for what?

**The forced trade-off** — put Round 1's contradiction directly to them:

> *"Two systems. One does exactly three things and nothing else. The other does twenty things, but
> you only need three of them. Which would you rather have, and why?"*

- What would make you call a system "too complicated"? Give me an example.

---

### SECTION 7 — Capacity and growth *(5 min)*

- What's the busiest week you've ever had? **What broke?**
- What did you do about it?
- If orders doubled next month, what's the first thing that would fail?
- Have you ever had to turn work away because you couldn't handle it?
- **Have you ever had to replace something because the business got bigger?** Tell me about it.
- Anything planned for the next year — new location, more staff, new product line?
- *(Do not ask whether that plan changes what they need from their systems. Note only whether they
  make the connection themselves.)*

---

### SECTION 8 — Money *(8 min)* — **LAST, DELIBERATELY**

#### 8.1 Behavioural first

- **What's the largest single amount you've spent on the business in the last two years, other than
  stock, rent, vehicles or salaries? What was it?**
- Was it worth it? How did you decide it was worth it?
- Who did you talk to before spending it?
- Do you pay anything monthly for the business besides rent and salaries?

#### 8.2 Then the anchored ladder — using *their* problem, not a generic one

> *"Suppose someone could reliably solve [their specific problem, in their own words], so that
> [the specific outcome they described]. What would that be worth to you?"*

**Let them name a figure first. Never anchor before they do.** Then walk the ladder and watch where
the reaction changes:

- If it cost **₦500,000** — what goes through your mind?
- **₦1,000,000**?
- **₦2,000,000**?

**The finding is not the number. It is the boundary** — where the reaction moves from "expensive
but maybe" to "not possible." Record the exact words at each step.

- At ₦1,000,000 — what would you need to see or know before handing that over?
- Who else would have to agree before you spent ₦1m?

#### 8.3 Structured trade-offs

Each isolates one variable. Ask all four:

| Trade-off | What it tests |
|---|---|
| ₦1,000,000 once **vs** ₦100,000/month for a year | Payment-model preference and cash-flow reality |
| **₦600,000 for something you fully own and control vs ₦300,000 for something the provider keeps control of** | **The ownership willingness-to-pay test.** A trade-off, never a preference question |
| ₦400,000 delivered in three months **vs** ₦800,000 delivered in three weeks | Urgency vs price |
| ₦700,000 for something built only for you **vs** ₦250,000/year for something many businesses use | Bespoke vs productised — tests Round 1's C2 and C5 contradictions |

#### Ethics for this section

State plainly at the start of Section 8: *"I'm not selling you anything and I'm not quoting you —
I'm trying to understand how businesses like yours think about this kind of money."* Take no
money, no deposits, no commitments. Do not ask for revenue figures — use the behavioural proxies
above, which are less intrusive and more accurate.

### SECTION 9 — Close *(2 min)*

- If you were advising me — what should a technology company **never** do to a business like yours?
- Anything I should have asked you and didn't?
- Who else should I talk to? *(Useful, but see the referral-bias warning in section 19.)*

## 5 — Behavioural test principles

The single design change from Round 1. Round 1 asked good questions and still collected opinions
where behaviour was available.

| Ask this | Not this |
|---|---|
| "Whose name is your domain registered in?" | "Do you value owning your systems?" |
| "What's the last app you stopped using, and why?" | "Is simplicity important to you?" |
| "What's the largest amount you've spent on the business that wasn't stock or rent?" | "Would you pay ₦1m?" |
| "Walk me through how you work out last month's profit." | "Do you struggle with your numbers?" |
| "What broke the last time you got busy?" | "Would you like software that grows with you?" |
| "Show me where that information lives." | "How do you keep records?" |
| "Have you ever changed providers? What did you pay again?" | "Would lock-in bother you?" |

**The rule:** if a question can be answered agreeably by someone who has never thought about the
subject, it is the wrong question.

## 6 — Pricing / willingness-to-pay approach

Round 1's largest gap: **no budgets, no figures, nobody asked.** Every pricing position YBMA holds
is currently an assumption.

### The four-layer approach

**Layer 1 — Revealed capacity (strongest).** What have they actually spent discretionarily?
Section 8.1 and screen question 5. A business that has never spent ₦200k on anything but stock,
rent, vehicles and salaries is unlikely to spend ₦1m, whatever it says.

**Layer 2 — Existing recurring spend.** What are they already paying monthly for software, apps
and services (Section 3)? This is the baseline against which any subscription would compete, and
it is far more informative than a hypothetical.

**Layer 3 — Problem-anchored valuation.** Price is meaningless in the abstract and meaningful
against a specific problem the respondent has just described in their own words (Section 8.2).
**Let them value it before we name a number.**

**Layer 4 — Structured trade-offs.** Four two-option choices (Section 8.3), each isolating one
variable: payment model, ownership, speed, bespoke-vs-productised.

### What each of the required questions is answered by

| Question the founders asked | How Round 2 answers it |
|---|---|
| What problems are worth ₦500k+? | Layer 3, cross-referenced against the problems scored highest in section 16 |
| What makes an SME comfortable spending that? | Section 8.2 — *"what would you need to see or know before handing that over"* — plus profile #1, the satisfied ₦500k+ buyer, who can answer it from experience |
| What makes ₦500k feel expensive / ₦1m reasonable / ₦2m unreasonable? | The ladder in 8.2. **Record the boundary, not the number** |
| What evidence do they need before spending? | Section 8.2 + Section 9 + profile #1 and #2 contrasted |
| Payment model preference | Trade-off 1, plus Layer 2's actual recurring-payment behaviour |

### One caution to write into the analysis

**Non-buyers' price answers are weak evidence and skew low.** Tag every price data point with the
respondent's purchase history and **never pool buyer and non-buyer pricing data into a single
statement.**

## 7 — Ownership test

Standing hypothesis **H22**: the question is no longer *"do customers value ownership"* but
**"can ownership be made legible to a buyer who does not know to ask?"**

### Three tests, in ascending order of evidential weight

**Test 1 — Literacy (weakest).** Do they know what they own?
*Whose name is the domain in? Who pays hosting? Do you have the logins? Who else has access?*
→ Establishes buyer literacy. **Does not establish that ownership matters.** Round 1: 3 of 5 could
not answer questions of this kind.

**Test 2 — Behaviour (strong).** Has ownership ever changed what they did?
- Have you ever **asked** a provider about access or ownership **before** paying?
- Have you ever **chosen or rejected** a provider because of it?
- Have you ever **paid more** for it?
- Have you ever **left** a provider over it?
→ This is the only category that distinguishes *"ownership sounds good"* from *"ownership changed
my purchasing behaviour."*

**Test 3 — Cost (strongest).** Has lock-in actually cost them?
- Have you ever paid twice for the same thing? How much?
- Have you ever lost access to something you paid for? What happened? What did it cost in money
  and in days?
- Have you ever been unable to change something because one person held it?
→ Quantified cost is the strongest available evidence. R1's abandonment was this class.

### The forced trade-off

**₦600,000 fully owned vs ₦300,000 provider-controlled.** This is the decisive instrument. If most
respondents take the ₦300,000 option while understanding the difference, ownership is not a
purchase driver — it is a contract clause worth having and not a brand idea.

### Things that must never be counted as evidence

- Agreement with *"ownership is important"*
- Agreement with *"you should own what you pay for"*
- Any positive response to a question that contains the word "own"

**Everyone agrees with all of these.** They are socially costless. Only Tests 2 and 3 count.

## 8 — Visibility test

Round 1's strongest signal — 5 of 5 mentioned it, 3 of 5 named it their biggest constraint — and
the one we know least about, because it emerged rather than being tested.

**The question is not whether it exists. It is whether it is a symptom or a product opportunity.**

### The six-part test

| # | Test | Question | What it establishes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Which information is hard to get** | Stock, last week's revenue, receivables, profit, who owes what | Scope of the problem |
| 2 | **How they get it now** | *"Walk me through it."* + **show me** | Effort cost. An answer of *"I just know"* is a very different finding from twenty minutes with a calculator |
| 3 | **How often they need it** | Daily? Weekly? Only at month end? | Frequency drives value |
| 4 | **What decisions depend on it** | What do you do differently when you know vs when you don't? | Whether the information is actually *used* |
| 5 | **What happens without it** | *"When was the last time a number surprised you? What did that cost?"* | **Economic consequence — the key discriminator** |
| 6 | **Have they tried to solve it** | Tried what? Paid what? What happened? | **The decisive test** |

### The discriminator, stated plainly

> **A problem that people complain about but have never tried to solve is a symptom.
> A problem people have already spent money or effort trying to solve is a market.**

Test 6 is therefore the one that matters. Round 1 did not ask it. Round 2 must.

### A caution on record

`[FACT]` **2 of 5 Round 1 respondents named primary constraints software cannot address** — capital
(R2) and customer demand (R3). If Round 2 reproduces that, visibility may be a real but secondary
problem sitting beneath constraints YBMA cannot sell into. Record every respondent's *first-named*
constraint separately from their *mentioned* constraints, because the distinction carries most of
the signal.

## 9 — Simplicity test

Round 1: 4 of 5 raised it, decisive for 2, and it was absent from three sessions of strategy.

### Decomposing "simple"

Do not assume it means fewer features. The candidate meanings, each with its own test:

| Candidate meaning | How to test it |
|---|---|
| **Few features** | The forced trade-off (three things vs twenty) |
| **Fast onboarding** | *"Last app you learned — how long before you were comfortable?"* R4's stated ceiling was one hour |
| **Minimal training** | *"If a new staff member started Monday, how long before they could use it?"* |
| **Familiar interface** | *"What does it remind you of?"* — WhatsApp is the likely reference point |
| **Mobile-first** | *"Do you do business things on your phone or a computer? Which for what?"* |
| **Speed** | *"Is anything you use slow? What do you do when it is?"* |
| **Few steps** | Observe: count the steps to record a sale in their current method |
| **Easy reporting** | *"What's the hardest thing to find out from what you use now?"* |
| **Easy support** | *"When something breaks, who do you call? What happens?"* |
| **Something else** | Section 9's open question |

### Resolving the Round 1 contradiction — C1

**R4** would reject software with *"too many things I don't need."*
**R5** outgrew his system and *"eventually needed more features."*

**Is this a segmentation line?** The hypothesis to test: simplicity preference tracks **business
stage and staff count** — solo operators want less, multi-staff operations want more. Three ways
to test it:

1. **Cross-tabulate** the forced-trade-off answer against staff band (1–3 / 4–6 / 7–10). The sample
   is deliberately balanced across the three bands for exactly this purpose.
2. **Cross-tabulate** it against prior technology experience.
3. Ask the C1 question **directly** of the two respondents who most resemble R4 and R5 —
   profiles #11 and #6.

**If the split tracks staff count or experience, it is segmentation and it is important.** If it is
random, it is individual preference and should be treated as a product decision rather than a
strategic one.

### One thing to record regardless

`[INFERENCE]` Every vendor claims ease of use. Even if simplicity scores highly on evidence, it may
be a **product requirement rather than a brand differentiator.** Score those two things separately
in section 16 — high customer relevance and low differentiation potential is a perfectly coherent
and useful result.

## 10 — Capacity test

Round 1 verdict: **part real phenomenon, part our construction.** Both respondents with systems
outgrew them; nobody expressed wanting to buy headroom.

### The single question that decides it

> **Does anyone connect a growth plan to a systems need *without being prompted*?**

Section 7 asks about growth plans and **deliberately stops there.** If the respondent then says
something like *"…and I'll need something better for that,"* that is direct evidence. If the
interviewer has to ask, it is not.

### The supporting behavioural tests

- Have you ever **paid more** for something because you expected to grow? *(Behaviour, not
  intention.)*
- Have you ever **replaced** something because the business got bigger? What did the replacement
  cost, and how did it compare to what you first paid?
- What broke the last time you got busy? *(Present-tense capacity evidence, not projection.)*
- Have you ever had to **turn work away**? *(The clearest possible economic consequence of a
  capacity limit.)*

### The falsification signal already on record

`[FACT]` R2: *"Right now, I can still manage most things myself."* R4: *"If I get more customers,
definitely."* Both describe buying **when the need arrives.** If three more respondents say the
same, capacity is settled as a non-factor at purchase — worth knowing, and cheap to establish.

### One reframe worth testing

`[HYPOTHESIS]` Profile #6 (outgrew a system) and #7 (currently frustrated) can answer a sharper
question than "do you want headroom": **what did replacing it cost you, compared with what buying
it right the first time would have cost?** If respondents can quantify the cost of replacement,
capacity stops being a preference and becomes an economic argument — which is a different and much
stronger thing. This is the one place where capacity could still become commercially meaningful.

## 12 — Jobs-to-be-done framework

Complete one of these for **every respondent**, immediately after the interview. Seven fields, and
**every field must carry an evidence class** (section 16).

### The record

| Field | What goes in it | Evidence rule |
|---|---|---|
| **Current situation** | What they do now, concretely. From Section 1 and 3 | Prefer observed |
| **Problem** | In *their words*, not ours. Quote it | Must be a quote or an observation |
| **Consequence** | What it costs — money, hours, lost customers, stock | Must carry a number or be marked UNKNOWN |
| **Current workaround** | Notebook, Excel, WhatsApp, staff member, memory, nothing | Prefer observed |
| **Desired outcome** | What "solved" looks like to them | Their words |
| **What they have already tried** | Every attempt, paid or unpaid, and what happened | **The pain test** |
| **What they would pay to change it** | Their own figure first, then their reaction to the ladder | Tag with purchase history |

### The rule that makes this work

**Every field must be traceable to something said or observed. A field that can only be filled by
inference is marked INFERENCE and excluded from any count.**

### What we are looking for

**Recurring jobs**, stated as business problems, that appear across sectors — because a job that
appears in a boutique, a clinic and a logistics operation is a product, while a job that appears in
one sector is a project.

And a deliberate discipline: **record the job without naming the technology.** *"I don't know what
I have in stock and it costs me sales"* is a job. *"I need an inventory system"* is a solution, and
accepting it as the job forecloses the very question the founders' service philosophy
(*"whatever technology solves the client's actual business problem"*) is designed to keep open.

## 13 — Buying-trigger research

The question: what moves a business from *"I have a problem"* to *"I will spend ₦500k–₦2m"*?

**Do not present the candidate list to respondents.** Find the trigger by reconstructing the
timeline.

### The reconstruction sequence — for every respondent who has bought or is buying

1. **"Take me back to before you bought it. What was happening in the business?"**
2. "When did you first think about doing something? What made you think about it then?"
3. **"Was there a specific moment, or a specific thing that happened?"**
4. "How long between that moment and actually paying?"
5. "What happened in between?"
6. "Was there anything that nearly stopped it?"
7. "Who else was involved in deciding?"

The gap between step 3 and step 4 is the **buying window** — and knowing how long it is matters
commercially, because it tells YBMA how long it has to be findable after a trigger fires.

### For profile #8 and #9 — the live buyers

These two are in the window right now, which makes them the most valuable trigger evidence
available:

- What made you start looking *now* rather than last year?
- What have you done so far — who have you spoken to?
- What would make you not go ahead?
- What's the deadline, if there is one? What happens if you miss it?

### How to record triggers

Log the trigger in the respondent's own words, then classify **afterwards** — never before. The
founders' candidate list (growth, lost revenue, operational chaos, customer complaints, competitor
pressure, manual workload, expansion, staff increase, new location, compliance, professionalisation,
investor/partner requirements) is a **coding frame for analysis, not a prompt for interviews.**

Also record, for each trigger: **who noticed it** — the owner, a member of staff, a customer, or an
outside party such as a bank, auditor or partner. `[HYPOTHESIS]` Externally-imposed triggers
(a bank, a contract, a regulator, a partner) may command larger budgets than internally-noticed
ones, because they carry a deadline and a consequence. Worth testing.

## 14 — Current alternatives / real competition

**Potentially more important than studying other agencies**, as the founders correctly noted.
YBMA's real competitor in Round 1 was a notebook.

### For each alternative in use, establish four things

> **Why they chose it · what it does well · what it fails to do · what would make them switch**

The third and fourth are the commercially useful ones and the ones that get skipped.

| Alternative | What to ask | What we are looking for |
|---|---|---|
| **Notebook / paper** | Show me. How long have you used it? What happens if it's lost or wet? Who else can read it? | `[FACT]` R4: *"Everything is in my head and my book."* R1 **reverted** to this after abandoning a system — the notebook beat paid software. Understanding why is essential |
| **Excel / Google Sheets** | Who built it? Can you change it? What breaks? Who else uses it? Is it on the phone or a computer? | The closest substitute to what YBMA would build, and the cheapest |
| **WhatsApp** | What exactly do you use it for — orders, payments, records, catalogue? How do you find an old order? | Almost certainly the highest-usage tool in the market. **Anything YBMA builds is competing with, or must sit alongside, WhatsApp** |
| **Existing SaaS** (POS software, Shopify, Paystack, Selar, QuickBooks, Zoho) | What do you pay? What made you pick it? What can't it do? Did you consider having something built instead? | Establishes the real price ceiling. If a ₦15,000/month product does 80% of the job, a ₦1m build must justify the difference |
| **Family member or staff member** | Who set it up? Are they still around? What happens when they're not? What do you pay them? | `[HYPOTHESIS]` This may be the single most common alternative to hiring a firm, and it is invisible in most research |
| **A freelancer** | How did you find them? What do you pay? Are they still reachable? | The direct competitor at this price point — and profile #13 gives us the supply-side view of the same relationship |
| **An agency** | Who? What did they charge? What happened? | Rare at this size. Worth knowing if it occurs |
| **Doing nothing** | What makes it tolerable? What would have to happen before you did something? | **The largest competitor of all, and the one most often left unstudied** |

### The question to ask about every alternative

> **"What would have to happen before you'd stop using that?"**

That answer is the switching condition, and it is more actionable than any statement of preference.

## 15 — What YBMA should test about its own offering

The founders' service philosophy — *"whatever technology solves the client's actual business
problem"* — is strategically sound and commercially difficult to communicate. Round 2 should test
the offering, not assume it.

### The four service questions

| Question | How to test it |
|---|---|
| **Is a generic website too low-value for a ₦500k–₦2m relationship?** | Profile #12 — what did they pay for a website, what did they get, would they pay 3–4× more, and for what? Plus: across all 13, does *anyone* raise a website unprompted? Round 1: zero of five |
| **Is custom software too expensive for parts of this segment?** | The ladder (8.2) shows where the wall is, per respondent. Cross-tabulate the wall against staff band, sector and revealed discretionary spend |
| **Can consulting be sold without credibility?** | *"Have you ever paid anyone just for advice about the business — no product, just advice?"* `[INFERENCE]` Most will say no, and that is the answer. Follow-up for buyers: *"Did you pay separately for the thinking, or only for the building?"* |
| **Is "digital transformation" too abstract?** | The language test below |

### The language test — do this with every respondent

Near the end of Section 9, read five descriptions of the same company and ask two questions.

> 1. *"We do digital transformation for small and medium businesses."*
> 2. *"We build software for businesses."*
> 3. *"We help you know what's actually happening in your business."*
> 4. *"We sort out your records, your stock and your money."*
> 5. *"We build the systems your business runs on."*

Then:
- **"Which of those would make you pick up the phone?"**
- **"Which of those means nothing to you?"**

This is cheap, takes ninety seconds, and is unusually valuable: it tests customer-facing language
directly **without pre-committing to a positioning.** Record the exact ranking per respondent, and
cross-tabulate against technology experience — `[HYPOTHESIS]` experienced buyers may respond to
different language than never-buyers.

Two cautions. Do **not** treat the winning phrase as a positioning — it is one signal among many,
and stated preference for a sentence is weak evidence. And **rotate the order** between interviews,
because whichever is read first has an advantage.

### One more offering question worth adding

**Bespoke or productised?** Trade-off 4 in Section 8.3 (₦700,000 built only for you vs ₦250,000/year
for something many businesses use) tests Round 1's C2 and C5 contradictions directly. R3 wanted
control and independence; R5 refused to be *"the first person testing something."* Those imply
opposite business models, and this is the cheapest way to find out which one this market rewards.

## 16 — Predefined analysis framework

**Written before the interviews so it cannot be adjusted afterwards to fit the result.** This is the
main defence against four sessions of accumulated confirmation bias.

### Evidence classification

Every claim in the Round 2 analysis must carry exactly one class.

| Class | Definition | Weight |
|---|---|---|
| **DIRECT BEHAVIOUR** | They did it, or it happened to them. Money spent, provider changed, system abandoned, work turned away, artefact observed | **Highest.** R1's abandonment was this class |
| **DIRECT STATEMENT** | Said plainly, about their own situation, **unprompted** | High |
| **INDIRECT SIGNAL** | Implied by something else, or stated only after prompting | Moderate. Record *where in the interview* it appeared |
| **INFERENCE** | Our reading. **Must be labelled and must never be included in any count** | None |
| **UNKNOWN** | Not asked, or not answered. **Must be recorded, never silently dropped** | None — but its absence is itself information |

> **"Sounds strategically attractive" is not an evidence class.** Any claim without a classified
> evidence line is deleted from the analysis, however useful it would be.

### Score the problems, not the hypotheses

**This is the most important structural rule.** Score each **job/problem** that emerges. Only then
ask which hypothesis the highest-scoring problems support. Scoring hypotheses directly invites
fitting evidence to theory — which is how "capacity" survived three sessions.

For each candidate problem, score **0–3** on ten dimensions:

| Dimension | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Problem frequency** | Never raised | 1–2 respondents | 3–5 | 6+ |
| **Problem severity** | Annoyance | Costs time | Costs money, respondent estimates it | Has caused a real loss or crisis they can describe |
| **Current workaround** | None needed | Works fine | Works but effortful | Visibly failing |
| **Economic consequence** | None stated | Vague | Estimated by respondent | **Quantified with a figure** |
| **Existing spending** | Nothing | Free tools only | Paid something small | **Paid ₦100k+ on this problem** |
| **Willingness to switch** | Content | Curious | Actively looking | **Already trying to fix it** |
| **Willingness to pay** | Rejects all prices | Under ₦500k only | ₦500k–₦1m reads as reasonable | **₦1m+ reads as reasonable** |
| **Urgency** | Someday | This year | This quarter | Now |
| **Recognisability** | Needs explaining | Understands once explained | Recognises it when described | **Names it themselves, first, unprompted** |
| **Differentiation potential** | Everyone claims it | Some claim it | Few claim it | **Nobody claims it, or competitors actively won't** |

Record **customer relevance and differentiation potential separately.** A problem can score high on
relevance and low on differentiation — simplicity is the likely case — and that is a coherent and
useful finding, not a contradiction.

### Four required bias controls

1. **Two-coder rule.** Two founders independently code each transcript **before** discussing it.
   Disagreements are recorded, not resolved by seniority. Systematic disagreement is itself a
   finding about the ambiguity of the evidence.
2. **Devil's advocate.** One founder is assigned, in advance, to argue against whichever hypothesis
   is leading at the debrief. Rotate the assignment.
3. **Quote-first write-up.** Every finding leads with a verbatim quote or an observed behaviour. If
   neither can be found, it is not a finding.
4. **Record the absences.** Maintain an explicit *"not mentioned by anyone"* list. Round 1's most
   useful single finding — nobody mentioned a website — came from an absence, and absences are
   invisible unless deliberately logged.

### Required outputs after Round 2

1. One Jobs-to-be-Done record per respondent (section 12)
2. The problem scorecard, all problems, all ten dimensions
3. The technology-experience comparison matrix (section 11)
4. The alternatives table (section 14)
5. The language-test rankings (section 15)
6. The not-mentioned-by-anyone list
7. Threshold outcomes — for each hypothesis: **ELEVATE / HOLD / DROP**, per section 17
8. The recruitment log, including everyone screened out and why

## 17 — Evidence thresholds

**Pre-registered. Set before the interviews. Not to be revised afterwards.**

Qualitative, not statistical — but specific enough to be decidable. Sample is 13; thresholds are
expressed as counts of respondents.

---

### VISIBILITY — *"is business visibility a major customer problem?"*

**ELEVATE** if all three hold:
- **6+ of 13** raise an information problem **unprompted in Section 1**, before Section 2 asks
- **4+** describe a **specific incident** where not knowing cost them money or stock
- **3+** have **already tried or paid** for something to fix it

**HOLD** if it is frequently mentioned but **nobody has tried to fix it** and nobody quantifies a
loss. → *A symptom, not a product opportunity.* This is the distinction that matters.

**DROP** if most say their current method works adequately, and the first-named constraints are
predominantly capital, demand, power or competition.

---

### OWNERSHIP — *"is ownership commercially meaningful?"*

**ELEVATE** if either holds:
- **2+** respondents have actually **changed, rejected, or paid more for** a provider because of
  access or control *(DIRECT BEHAVIOUR)*
- **3+** can **quantify a cost** incurred from lock-in — money paid twice, days or weeks lost

**HOLD** if it is recognised and resented **after prompting** but nobody has ever acted on it.
→ *A contract clause and a retention asset. Not a positioning.*

**DROP** if either holds:
- Buyers who understand lock-in say they would still take the cheaper option
- **The ₦600k-owned vs ₦300k-controlled trade-off goes to the cheaper option for 8+ of 13**

**Never counts:** agreement with *"ownership is important."*

---

### SIMPLICITY — *"is simplicity a meaningful differentiator?"*

**ELEVATE to differentiator** if both hold:
- **4+** describe **abandoning or not adopting** something specifically because of difficulty
  *(behaviour, not preference)*
- The forced trade-off splits along an **identifiable line** — staff band or technology
  experience — rather than randomly

**HOLD as a product requirement** if it is universally endorsed but nobody has abandoned anything
over it. → *Table stakes. Necessary, not distinguishing.*

**Note:** simplicity can pass the relevance test and fail the differentiation test. That outcome
should be recorded as such rather than resolved.

---

### CAPACITY — *"does capacity matter at the point of purchase?"*

**ELEVATE** if both hold:
- **3+** connect a growth plan to a systems need **unprompted** in Section 7
- **2+** report having **paid more or chosen differently** because of anticipated growth

**HOLD** if outgrowing is common retrospectively but nobody bought for it.

**DROP** if **5+** say some version of *"I'll deal with it when I get there."* R2 and R4 have
already said it; three more settles it.

---

### WILLINGNESS TO PAY ₦500k–₦2m — *the commercial existence question*

**SUPPORTED** if all three hold:
- **4+** have already spent **₦300k+** on something not essential to operations
- **3+** react to **₦1m** for their own named problem as *"expensive but possible"* rather than
  absurd
- **2+** have actually spent **₦500k+** on technology

**SERIOUS CONCERN** if the wall sits **below ₦500k for 8+ of 13.** → *Then the customer definition
and the price band are incompatible, and one of them has to move.* This is the most consequential
threshold in the document.

**Also record:** what distinguishes the respondents above the wall from those below it. That
distinguishing feature, if one exists, **is the real target definition.**

---

### THE STANDARD / "BUILT PROPERLY"

**ELEVATE** only if either holds:
- Respondents **spontaneously use quality or rigour language**
- **2+** describe having **paid more for better-built work**

**RETIRE PERMANENTLY as customer-facing** if Round 2 produces **zero** spontaneous quality language
across all 13 — as Round 1 did across all 5. It remains valuable as an internal operating standard
either way; this threshold concerns only whether it can ever be the customer-facing idea.

A clean, decidable test. Worth running once properly and then settling.

---

### Overall project threshold

**If Round 2 produces another set of contradictions as strong as Round 1's C1–C6, the honest
conclusion is that this is not one market** — and the correct next step is segmentation, not
positioning.

## 19 — Risks and sources of bias

| Risk | Why it matters here | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|
| **Referral-chain bias** | If respondents come from the founders' network or refer each other, profiles converge and criticism softens. A live suspicion about Round 1 — OQ11 is still unanswered | **Recruit at least half through a different route:** walk-in in a market or business district, a trade association, or a supplier who sells to many of them |
| **Interviewer effect** | The founders are researching a business they intend to build. Respondents will criticise "technology companies" freely but not the person in front of them | **Do not say you are building a technology company.** Say you are researching how small businesses run. Never pitch, never quote |
| **Social-desirability on money** | People overstate capacity to look successful, or understate it to deflect a sales pitch | **Anchor everything to past actual purchases**, not future intentions. Screen question 5 is the workhorse |
| **Hypothetical-purchase bias** | Non-buyers' price answers are unreliable and skew low — this distorted Round 1 | Sample is deliberately buyer-weighted (9 of 12). **Never pool buyer and non-buyer price data** |
| **Survivor bias** | You will only meet businesses that survived. Those that failed after a bad technology purchase are invisible, and they may be the most informative cases of all | Acknowledge as an unfixable limitation. Partly addressed via profile #13, who has seen clients fail |
| **Sector confound** | A Lagos–Kaduna difference could be city or sector | **The three matched pairs** (section 3) |
| **Leading and priming** | Round 1's Q7 introduced the ownership concept and weakened two respondents' evidence | Ownership moved to Section 5, after four sections of opportunity to arise unprompted. **Record first-mention position for every theme** |
| **Our own confirmation bias** | Four sessions and five hypotheses of accumulated investment | **Pre-registered thresholds** (17), **score problems not hypotheses** (16), two-coder rule, devil's advocate |
| **Over-fitting 13 respondents** | 13 is better than 5 and still not a market | No percentages. No *"Nigerian MSMEs want…"* Counts as *n of 13* only |
| **Language-test over-reading** | Stated preference for a sentence is weak evidence and easy to over-interpret | Rotate order between interviews. Treat as one signal. **Never treat the winner as a positioning** |
| **Mid-interview sales temptation** | Profiles #8 and #9 are **actively buying right now.** This is a genuine and immediate risk | **Decide in advance.** Recommendation: no selling during the interview; offer a separate follow-up afterwards. Note that converting a respondent compromises them for later rounds — and if the founders would rather sell to them, say so now and recruit different respondents for research |
| **Guide length fatigue** | 60 minutes is long for a business owner mid-shift | Book a specific time. Offer to come at a quiet hour. Section 18's cut order protects the essential sections |

# What Round 2 Must Answer

Eight questions. The round succeeds or fails on these.

1. **Is there a real overlap between businesses with 1–10 staff and businesses that will spend
   ₦500,000–₦2,000,000 on technology — and if so, what distinguishes them from the rest?**
2. **What job, stated as a business problem, is worth that much money to them?**
3. **What triggers the decision to spend it, and how long is the window between trigger and
   payment?**
4. **Is "not knowing what's happening in my business" a problem people have already spent money
   trying to solve — or one they merely complain about?**
5. **Has anyone ever changed, rejected, or paid more for a provider because of ownership and
   access — or does everyone take the cheaper option once the trade-off is explicit?**
6. **What are these businesses actually using instead of hiring a firm, and what would make them
   stop?**
7. **Does prior technology experience predict buying behaviour, trust requirements and ownership
   concern better than sector, size or city does?**
8. **Does anyone spontaneously value build quality — or is Round 1's silence on it the permanent
   answer?**
